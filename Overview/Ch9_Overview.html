Chapter 9: Accommodation and Enhancement Benefits

Benefits that neither insure you nor pay you for time away. They help you cope, care for your family, learn, and get to work. The hardest question about them is whether they pay for themselves.

Big idea: These benefits attack one problem from four directions: anything that pulls an employee's attention or attendance away from work. That means health, family, skills, and daily logistics. Because the payoff is indirect (less absence, better focus, longer tenure), the central skill in this chapter is asking what evidence shows this pays off, and how good that evidence is.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

The textbook's Chapter 9 runs on 2019–2020 data and 2020 tax limits. Its transit exclusion ($270 a month), its note that student loan assistance is tax-free only "through 2025," and its picture of the EEOC's wellness rules have all changed. A 2025 tax law altered several of these benefits, and the research behind the textbook's wellness figures has since been tested in randomized trials.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (IRS, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, peer-reviewed studies, and court and agency actions) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: The Big Picture

Why an employer would pay for any of this

As discretionary benefits, accommodation and enhancement benefits promote one or more of four objectives:

Well-being

The mental and physical health of employees and their family members: employee assistance and wellness programs.

Family assistance

Help caring for young children and dependent older relatives, and adoption support.

Education

Tuition help, scholarships, financial education, and student loan repayment.

Daily living

Support for the practical logistics of a working life: commuting and fitness.

Three reasons the business case is made

Absenteeism and tardiness cost less when employees' health, family, and transportation problems are handled.

Many employees are not fully productive for health-related reasons. Employers sponsor smoking cessation, alcohol treatment, and weight-control programs to help.

Education pays twice. Tuition benefits help employees compete for better jobs, and employers gain staffing flexibility and fewer obsolete skills.

Where it came from

Drinking on the job was widely tolerated early in the 20th century, and industrialization made the costs visible: low productivity, inefficiency, and accidents. In the 1940s companies shifted from a punitive to a rehabilitative stance, for three reasons. Wartime production required hiring people who would not otherwise have been employed. Stressful conditions pushed some workers to drink. And returning veterans, many affected by the stress of war, were reemployed. Those early alcohol intervention programs became the foundation of modern employee assistance programs. Wellness benefits have grown since, as the stigma around mental health has eased and employers have tied good health to lower health care and workers' compensation costs. The pandemic pushed the same benefits (mental health, family assistance, financial education) into more workplaces, all aimed at helping employees cope with stress and uncertainty.

Part Two: Employee Assistance Programs

Help before a problem becomes a termination

Employee assistance programs (EAPs) help employees cope with personal problems that can impair their lives or job performance: alcohol or drug abuse, domestic violence, depression, eating disorders, and the emotional effects of serious illness. The textbook's case for them rests on an estimate that about 20% of employees face difficulties that interfere with their work, costing over $575 billion a year in lost productivity. It also points to the offsetting benefits: lower turnover, absenteeism, health premiums, workers' compensation rates, accident costs, and disability costs. Treat those figures as the book's estimates. It does not give a current primary source for them.

The federal push: the Drug-Free Workplace Act of 1988. It requires federal contractors and grantees to maintain a drug-free workplace as a condition of the award. The textbook gives a $100,000 contract threshold, which is outdated: the Act's trigger follows the federal simplified acquisition threshold, which has been raised well above that. Workplace drug positivity has not fallen the way the book implies. The Quest Diagnostics Drug Testing Index reported 4.4% urine positivity in 2024 (down from 4.6% in 2023), and it has stayed at or above 4% for a decade. Marijuana is the most commonly detected drug.Updated

What an EAP does

  • Orientation for managers and employees on services and referral procedures.
  • Problem identification and assessment through an interview.
  • Short-term counseling instead of long-term therapy.
  • Referrals to professionals and community resources when short-term help is not enough.
  • Follow-up meetings, and crisis hotlines for urgent needs.
  • Outplacement assistance for laid-off or terminated employees: counseling, career assessment, job-search training, and résumé help. It also protects the company's image among departing workers and their families.

Two delivery models

Referral EAPs

  • A list of professional help services, from crisis lines to treatment programs
  • Usually no contract with providers, so cheapest and simplest
  • Large employers may negotiate a reduced rate from volume referrals

Full-service EAPs

  • In-house: company staff, often placed outside the workplace to encourage use
  • Third-party: an independent provider under a contract that sets services and costs
  • Consortium: smaller employers pool resources to contract with a provider

Getting employees to use it

Employees join by self-referral or on someone else's referral. Barriers are real: employees may not see how problems affect their work, may distrust employer sponsorship, may think personal problems should stay private, or may feel deep shame. Before referring an employee, a supervisor should use constructive confrontation: discuss specific incidents of poor performance and state that performance must improve within a set period. Supervisors describe the signs of trouble but never diagnose. The EEOC advises that a formal referral should be made only when an employee appears suicidal or has threatened workplace violence, and should otherwise be a matter of agreement with the employee.

Confidentiality: the make-or-break rule

Employees will not use an EAP that they think reports to their boss. Their greatest fears are job loss, lost promotions or raises, and reputational damage. That does not mean the EAP can never disclose anything. It must obtain written consent before releasing information to supervisors, treatment facilities, or family members. Every state, however, requires disclosure to the proper authorities without consent in certain cases, including suspected child abuse or neglect and evidence that someone poses a risk to their own health or safety or that of others.

Employer liability

Employers can be legally responsible for an EAP's mistakes, just as health professionals can be. The trouble spots are misdiagnosis that sends an employee to the wrong facility, negligent referral to a provider without proper credentials, abandonment (treatment stops early, or the employee's termination cuts off further treatment), inappropriate relationships between provider and client, and defamation from statements made because of EAP participation.

Which benefits laws apply?

ERISA. Not every EAP is subject to its reporting and disclosure rules. A referral-only EAP, where the employer neither funds services directly nor buys insurance for them, is exempt.

COBRA. Applies if the EAP is an ERISA welfare plan or a health care plan, for example because it provides counseling for a medical condition.

The Affordable Care Act. A comprehensive EAP that provides medical care is health coverage, subject to the ACA's group health plan rules, unless it qualifies as an excepted benefit. To qualify, an EAP must (1) provide no significant medical care, (2) not be coordinated with the group health plan (no requirement to exhaust the EAP first, or to enroll in the health plan to use it), (3) charge no employee premium, and (4) impose no cost sharing.

Programs are evaluated by asking whether troubled employees know the EAP exists, whether supervisors know how to refer, whether benefits outweigh costs, and whether to modify, keep, or drop it. The Employee Assistance Professionals Association offers an evaluation system based on interviews, peer review, and case analysis. The textbook notes it is hard to put a dollar value on helping people, which is one reason the evidence problem in the next section matters.

Part Three: Wellness Programs

Promoting health: does it work?

Wellness programs promote the physical and psychological health of employees, on site or off. The textbook's list:

Back care

Worker training, exercise programs, ergonomic evaluations, and relaxation techniques, aimed at the shoulder and back injuries that drive absence.

Smoking cessation

From information campaigns to intensive programs, usually on a reimbursement basis. The employer deducts its contributions as a business expense.

Stress management

Helps employees cope with stressors at work and outside it. Gains: productivity, attendance, and lower health costs. Its importance grew sharply during the pandemic.

Weight control and nutrition

Program memberships, support groups, healthier vending options, and on-site screenings for diabetes, blood pressure, and cholesterol.

Health risk appraisals

Questionnaires and exams that flag potential health risks. When integrated with an EAP, results can steer employees to confidential help.

The evidence, then and now

The textbook reports that medical costs fall by about $3.27 and absenteeism costs by $2.73 for every dollar spent on wellness programs, and that employers spent $762 per employee on them in 2019. Those figures trace to a 2010 review of earlier studies that found roughly a three-to-one return. Most of those studies compared people who chose to join a program with people who did not, which is a problem: the healthy are more likely to sign up. Randomized trials have since tested the question directly.Updated

What the trials do and do not show. They do not prove that wellness programs are useless. They show that, in these two settings and time frames, a comprehensive program did not produce the savings that the earlier, nonrandomized studies suggested. Employees who join voluntarily are often the ones who need the program least, so simple comparisons overstate the effect. The Illinois authors report that their results rule out 84% of earlier estimates. The authors also stress that some questions remain open, such as effects over longer periods.

The law: what incentives are allowed?

ACA and HIPAA. Wellness programs that are part of a group health plan may offer incentives of up to 30% of the total cost of coverage, and up to 50% for programs aimed at reducing or preventing tobacco use, for health-contingent programs.

ADA and GINA. Programs that ask medical questions or require exams, or that involve genetic information such as family history, must be voluntary. The textbook describes the EEOC's 2016 rules, which set a 30% incentive limit under those laws. After AARP sued, a federal court found the EEOC had not justified that limit, and the incentive provisions were vacated effective January 1, 2019. The EEOC's January 2021 proposal for replacement rules was suspended, and the sources I reviewed show no replacement dollar limit. The requirement that participation be voluntary still applies, but employers have no bright-line incentive number under those two laws.Updated

Part Four: Family Assistance

Child care, elder care, and adoption

Family assistance programs help employees care for young children and dependent older relatives. The cheapest and least labor-intensive form is a referral service, delivered through workshops, newsletters, or the EAP. At the other end are on-site centers and subsidies for community day care.

Child care

  • Supervision of preschool-age children of working parents, with preschool preparation and meals
  • Driven by single parents and dual-career households
  • Only 13% of private workers have access (2025). The textbook's 11% counts companies, a different measure

Elder care

  • Physical, emotional, or financial help for aging parents, spouses, or relatives who are frail or disabled
  • Day programs typically offer meals and activities
  • Facilities usually need state or local licenses

New for 2026: three tax changes in the 2025 law.Updated The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 4, 2025) made these changes:

· The dependent care assistance exclusion, which applies to dependent care flexible spending accounts, rises from $5,000 to $7,500 starting in 2026 ($3,750 for married filing separately). It is not indexed to inflation.

· The employer-provided child care credit becomes 40% of qualified expenses, up to $500,000, for expenses from 2026 (50% and $600,000 for eligible small businesses), up from 25% and $150,000.

· Employers may make tax-free contributions of up to $2,500 a year to a new "Trump Account", an IRA-like account for a child under 18, for an employee or the employee's dependent. It requires a written plan and nondiscrimination testing similar to dependent care plans.

Adoption assistance

Adoption benefits fall into three types: information (referrals to agencies, support groups, and experts), financial assistance (a lump sum, or reimbursement of up to about 80% of fees), and parental leave that extends the FMLA to adoptive parents. Employers use them to compete for talent, to signal a family-friendly culture, and to give adoptive parents the same support biological parents get. The textbook's extreme example is Netflix's unlimited paid parental leave, and it gives one to three months as the norm. Qualified adoption expenses are the reasonable and necessary adoption fees, court costs, attorneys' fees, and similar costs of adopting an eligible child (under 18, or physically or mentally incapable of self-care). The textbook cites IRC Section 23. Expenses of a foreign adoption count only once the adoption is final.

The 2026 numbers: the employer-provided adoption assistance exclusion and the adoption credit are each $17,670 per child, phasing out for modified adjusted gross income between $265,080 and $305,080. Since 2025, up to $5,000 of the credit is refundable.Updated

Part Five: Education and Money

Learning benefits and financial help

The textbook says 83% of companies offer some educational benefit, and virtually all full-time employees, but only about a third of part-timers, qualify. Companies benefit, but many fail to build the career paths that let newly educated employees move up, so the employees leave for jobs elsewhere.

Educational assistance

Payments for tuition, fees, books, supplies, and equipment, undergraduate or graduate, work-related or not. Under a written, nondiscriminatory Section 127 program, employees can exclude up to $5,250 a year from income. That limit is unchanged in 2026. Amounts above it are taxable, unless they qualify as a working condition fringe (education that maintains or improves job skills, or is required by the employer or by law), which has no cap.

Tuition reimbursement

Full or partial reimbursement of education and training expenses. Many employers allow courses unrelated to the job. Do not confuse it with pay-for-knowledge, where set courses earn raises. Plans qualify as working condition fringes when the training is required or maintains job skills.

Scholarships

Cover tuition for employees or, usually, their children. The least common educational benefit, since employers gain less from educating employees' children. The tax rules are in IRC Section 117. Tuition reductions that discriminate in favor of highly paid employees lose favorable treatment.

Financial education

Resources for budgeting and long-term saving. The textbook says 21% of employers offer it, and 83% of HR professionals say financial troubles hurt performance. Five principles: know the people you serve; give actionable, timely information; build key skills; build on motivation; and make good decisions easy.

Student loan repayment help

The textbook lists four ways to structure it: a 401(k)-linked match (workers' loan payments trigger employer retirement contributions), signing bonuses that are taxable, direct payments to the loan servicer, and letting employees convert unused paid time off into loan payments (the last does not work in every state). It also says the tax exclusion for employer loan payments up to $5,250 lasted only through 2025.

That deadline is gone.Updated The 2025 law made the Section 127 exclusion for employer payments of employees' qualified education loans permanent. Loan repayment shares the same $5,250 annual cap with tuition help, and the cap will be indexed for inflation for tax years after 2026. An employer can therefore put loan help on the same footing as tuition help without worrying about expiration.

Part Six: Daily Living

Commuting and fitness

Transportation

Employers sponsor transportation services (transit subsidies, vanpools, employer vans or buses) to recruit people who dislike rush-hour driving or lack parking, and, in smoggy metropolitan areas, to comply with the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990 and their state and local commuter trip reduction laws. The IRC separates de minimis transportation benefits (so little in value that accounting for them is impracticable) from qualified transportation benefits (transit passes, vanpooling, and parking).

Two corrections to the book's wording. The tax benefit belongs to the employee as an exclusion from income, not a deduction. And the employer's own tax deduction for providing these benefits was eliminated in 2018, which makes them less attractive to employers than the book suggests.

Physical fitness

Employers support fitness through on-site centers or memberships at nearby clubs, expecting less absenteeism, better stress management, and lower health insurance claims. The employer deducts the cost as a business expense. Employees may exclude the value of using an on-premises athletic facility if it is on the employer's property, operated by the employer (or under its control by contract), and used almost entirely by employees, their spouses, and their dependent children. "On-premises" means the employer's property, not necessarily a business location.

Critical thinking

A wellness program built on a $3.27 promise

The situation. A 500-employee manufacturer's CFO wants to launch a wellness program. She cites a vendor deck, and the same figures in your textbook: medical costs fall $3.27 and absenteeism $2.73 for every dollar spent. The proposal is a biometric screening, a health risk appraisal, coaching, and a premium discount of up to 30% for employees who complete it and hit health targets. Using the book's 2019 average, the program would cost about $762 per employee, or roughly $381,000 a year. The company self-funds its health plan and has a workforce that is older, more male, and heavily hourly.

  1. Is the $3.27 figure a reliable basis for this decision? Explain what is wrong, or right, with relying on it.
  2. Identify at least two legal or ethical risks in the incentive design, and say how you would reduce each.
  3. Propose a way to test whether the program works before committing the whole budget, and state what result would lead you to stop.

Your response stays in your browser. It is not saved or submitted. Copy it into your course platform when you are done.

There is no single right answer. A strong response takes a position and shows it has weighed these points:

  • Selection bias. The 3-to-1 estimate comes from a 2010 review of largely nonrandomized studies, in which volunteers are healthier than nonparticipants. Two later randomized trials (a 160-worksite trial and a roughly 5,000-employee trial) found no significant effect on medical spending, productivity, or health outcomes, though screening rates and some behaviors improved. A vendor's return-on-investment claim deserves the same skepticism as any claim by a party that profits from it.
  • Will it transfer? Neither trial was a self-funded manufacturer with an older, hourly workforce. That is an argument for testing, not for assuming the results will or will not hold.
  • Incentive design risks. A health-contingent program is capped at 30% of the total cost of coverage under HIPAA/ACA rules and must offer a reasonable alternative standard to people who cannot meet a target. ADA and GINA require the medical inquiries to be voluntary, and there is no current EEOC bright line, so a large penalty for declining invites a challenge. Fairness matters as well: employees with disabilities or chronic conditions may be least able to hit targets, and hourly workers may have the least time to participate. Options include smaller incentives, participation-based rather than outcome-based rewards, and alternatives for anyone who cannot meet a target.
  • Privacy. Health risk appraisals and screenings collect sensitive data. Who sees it, and could it affect employment decisions? Employees' fears here are the same ones that undermine an EAP.
  • A credible test. Randomize the offer across sites or departments, or stagger the rollout so that some sites serve as controls. Track medical claims, absence, and turnover for at least 18 to 24 months, and compare participants and nonparticipants only against the randomized control group. Decide the stop rule in advance, for example no measurable change in claims or absence by the end of the period, and treat gains in screening alone as not enough to justify the full cost.
  • What else could the money do? A strong answer compares this against alternatives, such as better coverage, lower cost sharing, an EAP, or ergonomic changes that address the injuries actually driving absence.
