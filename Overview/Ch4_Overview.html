Part One: The Big Picture

The three-legged stool

The federal government describes retirement security as a three-legged stool. This chapter covers one leg in depth — the other two show up elsewhere in the course.

Employer-sponsored plans

This chapter. Plans your employer designs, funds, and administers.

Government benefits

Social Security retirement benefits — covered in Chapter 7.

Personal savings

What you set aside on your own, outside any employer or government program.

One trend is worth holding onto as you read: between the early 1990s and 2020, participation in defined benefit plans fell from 32% to just 11% of private-sector workers, while defined contribution plan participation rose from 35% to 47%. This chapter explains why that shift happened — and what it means for whoever's reading their retirement statement.

Qualified vs. nonqualified plans

Whether a plan meets every minimum standard set by ERISA determines its tax treatment — and who it's typically designed for.

Qualified plans

  • Meet all ERISA minimum standards
  • Immediate tax benefits for employer and employee
  • Typically cover nonexecutive employees

Nonqualified plans

  • Fail to meet at least one minimum standard
  • Used for nonqualified deferred compensation (NQDC)
  • Typically cover key or highly compensated employees

The rules qualified plans must follow

Participation: generally, employees must be at least 21 with one year of service — though the SECURE Act now requires access for long-term part-time employees too.

Vesting: employees earn non-forfeitable rights on a schedule — either cliff vesting (100% after up to three years) or a six-year graduated schedule.

Coverage: plans can't disproportionately favor highly compensated employees — tested by percentage, ratio, or average-benefit tests.

Nondiscrimination: plans must meet safe harbor conditions or pass facts-and-circumstances testing to avoid favoring high earners.

Top-heavy provisions: if key employees hold more than 60% of plan value, the plan must provide minimum benefits to everyone else.

Part Two: Two Main Designs

Defined benefit plans

A defined benefit plan guarantees a retirement payout — usually a percentage of pay multiplied by years of service. The employer bears the investment risk.

Flat benefit formula example

Employee averages $100,000/year over their final 3 years. Plan pays 60% of that average.

$100,000 × 60% = $60,000/year

Unit benefit formula example

Employee retires with 25 years of service and a $52,500 final average salary; the plan's earned percentage is 43.43%.

$52,500 × 43.43% = $22,800.75/year

To prevent employers from "backloading" benefits toward the end of a career, accrual rules like the 3% rule and the fractional rule require benefits to build up at a steady pace. If a plan fails and can't pay, the PBGC insures a portion of what's owed.

Defined contribution plans

Here, the employer and/or employee contribute to an individual account, and the eventual payout depends on contributions plus investment performance. The employee bears the investment risk — this is the design driving the shift you saw in the three-legged-stool section above.

401(k) / Roth 401(k)

Employees defer part of their pay; Roth version taxes contributions now, not withdrawals.

Profit-sharing plans

Employer contributions come from a pool tied to company profits.

Stock bonus / ESOPs

Paid in employer stock rather than cash; employees can vote as shareholders.

SIMPLE plans

Designed for small companies (100 or fewer employees) as IRAs or 401(k)s.

403(b) plans

Tax-deferred annuities available to nonprofit organizations.

457 plans

Typically for state or local government employees; only employees contribute.

The SECURE Act also created pooled employer plans, letting small, unrelated employers band together into a single 401(k) — improving their bargaining power on fees.

Hybrid plans

Hybrid designs blend features of both worlds — cash balance plans, pension equity plans, target benefit plans, and money purchase plans all combine a defined-benefit-style guarantee with a defined-contribution-style individual account.

Watch for: when a company converts a traditional pension into a cash balance plan, older workers can experience "wearaway" — a period where their benefit stops growing under the new formula. The Pension Protection Act addressed this directly, since it disproportionately hurt employees closest to retirement.

Don't just memorize the plan types.

Think about who's holding the risk.

Employer designs a plan → Contributions are set → Investments perform (or don't) → Risk lands on employer or employee → Retirement outcome

Every plan type in this chapter answers the same question differently: who bears the risk if the investments underperform? Keep asking that as you read.
