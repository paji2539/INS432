Part One: The Foundation

Why does government regulate benefits at all?

Congress generally avoids telling employers how to run their business. But when workers and employers aren't on equal footing, lawmakers have stepped in. In 1911, a fire at the Triangle Shirtwaist factory in New York City killed 150 workers — many trapped behind exit doors the company had locked. That tragedy helped set in motion decades of labor law aimed at rebalancing power between employers and employees.

That same logic runs through every law in this chapter: when the market alone doesn't protect workers, the law fills the gap.

Labor unions and the NLRA (1935)

The National Labor Relations Act restored bargaining power to employees by protecting their right to unionize. It covers most private-sector companies, but not agricultural or domestic workers, independent contractors, or government employees — the National Labor Relations Board enforces it. Once a union is in place, benefits-related topics fall into three bargaining categories:

Mandatory

Must negotiate

Disability pay, health care, paid time off, and retirement plans — if either side asks.

Permissive

May negotiate

Fund administration, retiree benefits, and workers' compensation.

Illegal

Cannot negotiate

Anything that would violate the NLRA or other federal/state law.

Unions were also behind the WARN Act, which requires 60 days' notice before mass layoffs or plant closings.

Tax code and wage law

The Internal Revenue Code (IRC) governs federal taxation — and offers tax incentives that make many benefits attractive for employers to offer in the first place.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA, 1938) sets minimum wage, overtime pay, and child labor rules. Because some benefits are tied to pay, they rise correspondingly during overtime hours.

Part Two: The Big Structure

ERISA: the law behind most benefits practice

The Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 regulates medical care, life and disability insurance, and retirement programs. It's detailed, far-reaching, and preempts most state law. It breaks into four titles:

Title I — Protection of employee rights

Reporting, disclosure, vesting, funding, and fiduciary duties.

Title II — IRC amendments

Tax treatment rules for pension and welfare benefits.

Title III — Jurisdiction and enforcement

Gives the Department of Labor power to investigate and enforce.

Title IV — Plan termination insurance

Created the PBGC, which insures roughly 25,000 pension plans covering over 34 million workers.

Three major amendments extended ERISA's reach: COBRA (1985) lets employees keep group health coverage after a qualifying event like job loss; HIPAA (1996) protects health plan portability and privacy; and the Pension Protection Act (2006) strengthened funding rules and made automatic 401(k) enrollment easier.

The modern era: ACA and SECURE Act

2010

Affordable Care Act

Requires employers with 50+ employees to offer affordable health coverage. Helped an estimated 20 million people gain coverage pre-pandemic. (More in Chapter 5.)

2020

SECURE Act

Expanded retirement plan access to long-term part-time employees and created pooled employer plans, letting small employers band together for better terms.

Equal-employment opportunity laws

Benefits aren't exempt from anti-discrimination law. The EEOC enforces a series of laws that apply just as much to benefits as to hiring and pay:

The through-line: the Equal Pay Act (1963), Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964), the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (1967), the Pregnancy Discrimination Act (1978), the ADA (1990), the Civil Rights Act (1991), and GINA (2008) all prohibit discrimination in employment practices — including who gets access to which benefits, and on what terms.
