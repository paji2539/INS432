Part One: Psychology

The employment relationship as exchange

At its core, the employer-employee relationship is a form of social exchange — the employer offers inducements (wages, benefits) in return for employee contributions (effort, commitment). That exchange happens on two levels at once.

Economic exchange

  • Terms specified at time of hire
  • Governed by explicit policy
  • Example: wages, health insurance

Social exchange

  • Evolves over the employment period
  • Not fully specified up front
  • Builds motivation and commitment over time

A shifting workforce — more contingent work, less job security, more diversity in needs — makes this exchange harder to standardize. It's also expensive: companies spend roughly 30% of total compensation on benefits alone.

Psychological contracts

A psychological contract is an employee's subjective sense of the mutual obligations between them and their employer. Unlike a wage agreement, it's implicit — and it forms mostly before day one, shaped by interactions with the company and perceptions of its culture.

TransactionalRelational

Transactional expectations are economic and extrinsic (pay, legally required benefits). Relational expectations are emotional and intrinsic (career growth, job security). Retirement plans and educational assistance sit in the middle — they touch both.

When contracts break: a violation happens when an employee perceives a gap between what was promised and what was delivered — through either reneging (the employer deliberately breaks a promise) or incongruence (employer and employee never shared the same understanding to begin with). Clear communication is the best defense against both.

Fairness, attitudes, and citizenship

When contracts are violated, employees experience it as unfairness. Perceptions of justice around benefits fall into four types:

Distributive

Is what I receive fair, given what I contribute?

Procedural

Was the process used to decide benefits fair?

Informational

Was I given accurate, timely explanations?

Interpersonal

Was I treated with respect along the way?

These perceptions, plus a sense of perceived organizational support, drive whether employees engage in organizational citizenship behavior — the discretionary, above-and-beyond effort that isn't required but helps the organization succeed. Employees who feel fairly treated give more of it. Employees who don't, withhold it.

Part Two: Economics

Why do employers offer benefits?

If cash were all that mattered, employers and employees might both prefer cash-only pay. Three economic rationales explain why benefits persist:

1

Cost advantage

Group insurance is cheaper per person than individual coverage — larger groups mean lower risk and lower administrative cost per employee.

2

Recruiting the right workers

Offering benefits like tuition reimbursement can attract candidates with the traits employers want, even traits a job application can't reveal.

3

Tax incentives

Many benefits aren't taxed as employee income, which the Internal Revenue Code rewards employers for offering.

Who actually pays for benefits?

Common misconception: benefits are a "free" gift from the employer. In practice, employees pay for their own benefits — through lower cash wages than they'd otherwise receive. When benefit costs rise, it's employees who absorb the cost, not employer profits.

How much of a cost increase gets passed on to employees depends on four factors:

The cash value employees place on the benefit

Whether employers change hiring as compensation costs shift

Whether the cost increase hits one employer or the whole market

Whether one employer's hiring decisions move the broader market rate
