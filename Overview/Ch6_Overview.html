Chapter 6: Employer-Sponsored Disability Insurance, Life Insurance, and Workers' Compensation

Three ways to protect a paycheck when the worker can't earn it. Two are choices the employer makes. One is a bargain the law forces.

Big idea: Every benefit in this chapter answers the same question: who pays when a worker can't work, or dies? The answer turns on two facts. Was the harm work-related? Does the law require the benefit? Workers' compensation is mandatory and covers only work-related harm. Disability and life insurance are voluntary and cover nearly everything else. Offset provisions keep the layers from adding up to more than the paycheck.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

The textbook's Chapter 6 runs on 2020 figures: access rates, per-employee costs, and a pandemic section describing COVID-19 rules that have since expired. Several of those figures have moved, and one of the government data series behind them is changing.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, the National Academy of Social Insurance, NCCI, the IRS, SSA, and the Texas Department of Insurance) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: The Big Picture

Three benefits, one question

Two of these benefits are discretionary: the employer decides whether to offer them. The third is required by law in every state but one. Keeping the three straight is most of the chapter.

Disability insurance

Replaces income when illness or injury keeps an employee from working. Covers work-related and nonwork-related causes, so it is broader than workers' comp. The employer buys a policy, pays the insurer a negotiated premium, or self-funds. Voluntary.

Life insurance

Pays a specified amount to the employee's beneficiaries at death. Usually a multiple of salary, with the option to buy more. Group coverage lets more people join at a lower cost per person. Voluntary.

Workers' compensation

Medical care and income for work-related injury, illness, or death, regardless of fault. Run by each state. Mandatory everywhere except Texas.

What they are not

Sick leave (Chapter 8) is a different thing. It pays for occasional minor absences out of the regular payroll, not from an insurance policy or a self-insured disability fund.

The need is real. SSA reports that a 20-year-old worker has a 1-in-4 chance of developing a disability before reaching full retirement age.Updated The textbook's version puts the odds of a 90-day disability at one in three. Both numbers make the same point: disability is not a rare event.

Where these benefits came from

  • Late 1800s. The U.S. shifts from farming to factories. Companies pay little attention to safety, and an injured worker's only remedy is a lawsuit few could afford.
  • The three common-law defenses. Employers who were sued could argue assumption of risk (the hazard was ordinary to the job), the fellow worker rule (a coworker caused it), or contributory negligence (the worker's own carelessness contributed). Workers lost most of the time.
  • 1911. The first workers' compensation law passes. By 1948 every state has one. The states, not Washington, built this system.
  • Before the 1960s. Disability coverage is primitive: establishment funds, individual policies, and early group policies that pay a lump sum that ignores age and severity.
  • 1960s. Insurers begin paying disability benefits as income replacement, the model still used today.
  • Life insurance. The same forces that drove workers' comp drove group life: industrialization, WWII wage freezes (the same accident behind employer health coverage in Chapter 5), and favorable tax rules.

The two principles behind workers' compensation. First, the employer is absolutely liable for occupational injuries, whoever was at fault. Second, the employer should bear the cost of those injuries. In exchange, the employer gets protection from most lawsuits. Hold on to that trade. It comes back in Part Four.

Part Two: Disability Plan Design

Short-term and long-term disability

Short-term disability (STD)

  • Covers disabilities lasting less than a year, with benefits usually paid for no more than a year
  • Definition: unable to perform any and every duty of your regular job
  • Covers recovery from injury or surgery, illness requiring hospitalization, and pregnancy or childbirth
  • Typically pays 50% to 66⅔% of pay, weekly or monthly. Some plans pay 100%

Long-term disability (LTD)

  • Picks up where short-term ends, lasting from six months to life
  • Two-stage definition (below)
  • Pays 50% to 60% of monthly pay, subject to a dollar maximum that may be as high as $10,000
  • Elimination period of six months to a year. Usually starts only after sick leave and STD run out

Both types may overlap with Social Security disability and workers' compensation rather than replace them. Employer plans supplement the legally required benefits. That overlap is the reason offset provisions exist.

Three features that decide whether a claim pays

Preexisting condition clause. Excludes any condition for which you received advice, diagnosis, care, or treatment during a designated period before coverage began. The period is usually any time before you enrolled. Insurers use it to avoid paying for problems that predate their coverage.

Waiting periods. The pre-eligibility period runs from your hire date until you may join the plan. The elimination period is the minimum time you must be disabled before payments start. Employers like long elimination periods because premiums are lower. STD elimination periods commonly run about three months, but can range from one month to a year.

Exclusions. Self-inflicted injuries are almost always excluded. Mental illness and chemical dependency are generally excluded too, though an employee assistance program may fill the gap.

Long-term disability: the two-stage definition

Stage one: "own occupation"

For a designated period, often up to two years, you qualify if the illness or accident prevents you from doing your own job. A surgeon who loses fine motor control qualifies, even if she could work as a teacher.

Stage two: "any occupation"

After that period, the test tightens. You qualify only if you cannot perform any occupation for which your training, education, or experience suits you. Many claims end at this transition.

LTD traditionally covered only total disability. Carriers have added partial disability coverage: while you work part-time in any job, the insurer covers part of the lost income. The plan may kick in when part-time earnings fall below a set share of prior income, say 75% to 80%. That helps employees ease back into work and helps employers retain them.

How employers fund disability coverage

Buy insurance

  • Pay premiums to an independent insurer, which takes on the risk
  • Predictable cost, minimal claims administration
  • Makes sense for small groups, where claims are hard to predict

Self-fund, fully or partly

  • Partial (mainly LTD): pay claims from a trust for 2–3 years or up to a maximum, and buy stop-loss insurance for the excess
  • Full: pay from company assets, or hire an insurer through an administrative-services-only (ASO) contract to run the plan
  • Depends on group size and tolerance for exposure, the expected annual claims

The logic echoes Chapter 5: larger groups make disability incidence predictable, and predictability makes self-funding attractive.

Part Three: The Laws and the Offsets

What shapes a disability plan

ADEA (1967) and OWBPA (1990). The Older Workers Benefit Protection Act generally bans terminating LTD benefits for active employees because of age. Employers may charge older workers more for benefits that cost more for them, but may not make it a condition of employment. The employee must be offered choices: withdraw, keep the premium the same with reduced coverage, or pay more to keep full coverage.

ADA (1990). Prohibits discrimination against a qualified individual with a disability, meaning a person who can perform a job's essential functions with or without reasonable accommodation. The EEOC enforces it. Employers violate the ADA by excluding eligible employees from a plan or by imposing a longer pre-eligibility period on them. But it is lawful to omit cost-of-living adjustments from disability retirement benefits, or to reduce benefits for other insured income.

ERISA (1974). Regulates how company-sponsored disability and life plans are established and run.

State workers' comp and Social Security disability rules. These interact with employer plans through offset provisions.

Offset provisions: the arithmetic of not being paid twice. An offset provision reduces the company-sponsored benefit by a percentage of what you receive from workers' comp and Social Security. Without one, a disabled worker could collect as much as, or more than, his paycheck, and the incentive to return to work would vanish. The textbook cites a combined limit: Social Security disability plus workers' compensation may not exceed 80% of average current earnings before the disability.

The public layer is not large. In January 2026 the average disabled worker on Social Security received about $1,630 a month, and a beneficiary who earns more than $1,690 a month from work generally loses eligibility.Updated That thin public floor is why an employer plan matters to a higher earner.

Part Four: Life Insurance

Group life insurance

Individuals can buy life insurance from an agent, or get group coverage through an employer. Group coverage is usually issued to a group of at least 10 full-time employees. It lets a larger pool join at a lower cost per person, with the employer financing all or part of it. Most plans also include accidental death and dismemberment coverage.

Three plan designs, plus AD&D

Term life

The most common employer plan. Protects beneficiaries only for a set period or up to a maximum age (often 65 or 70). It has no cash value. Nobody receives anything when it expires. To continue, you renew and keep paying premiums.

Whole life

Pays at death whenever it occurs. It never expires before payout, and combines insurance with a savings feature. Substantially more expensive than term, and uncommon in employer plans.

Universal life

Term protection plus a flexible savings account. The owner can shift money between the insurance and savings components. The cash value grows at a variable rate tied to markets, so premiums, benefits, and schedules can change.

Accidental death & dismemberment (AD&D)

Pays for death or dismemberment (loss of two limbs, or of sight) caused by an accident, not by illness. Premiums are lower because accidental deaths are rarer. Many employers now offer it as voluntary, employee-paid coverage.

Who pays: contributory vs. noncontributory

Contributory plans

  • Employees pay all of the premium, or share it with the employer
  • Less common in group term coverage

Noncontributory plans

  • The employer pays the entire premium for coverage up to set limits
  • Most group plans work this way, because the employer enjoys substantially larger tax benefits than with contributory plans

How an insurer prices it. Underwriting is the process of deciding whether to issue a policy, on what terms, and at what premium. It relies on mortality tables that give yearly probabilities of death by age and sex, established by the Society of Actuaries. That is the same logic you met in Chapter 5, applied to death instead of illness.

The tax rule the textbook barely mentions: imputed income

Employer-paid group term life is tax-free up to $50,000 of coverage. Above that, the IRS treats the value of the excess as income to you, even though you never receive cash. The value is not your employer's real premium. It comes from a fixed IRS table (the textbook's Exhibit 6.1) based on your age on December 31.Updated The imputed amount is also subject to Social Security and Medicare tax.

Part Five: Workers' Compensation

The no-fault bargain

State workers' compensation laws replaced the old lottery of lawsuits with a trade. The worker gets prompt medical care and income benefits whoever was at fault. The employer gets protection from most lawsuits over the injury. The textbook lists six objectives behind that trade. The ones worth remembering:

  • Sure, prompt, reasonable benefits for accident victims or their dependents, regardless of fault.
  • A single remedy, which cuts court delays, legal fees, and litigation costs.
  • Employer interest in safety and rehabilitation, encouraged through experience-rated premiums.
  • Open study of accident causes rather than concealment of fault.

Who is covered

Workers' comp laws cover virtually every employee, except domestic workers, some agricultural workers, and workers at very small businesses in some states. Maritime workers and federal civilian employees have their own programs (the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act and the Federal Employees' Compensation Act). A compulsory law requires employers to accept the state system. An elective law lets them reject it, at the price of losing the common-law defenses described earlier.

The exception: Texas.Updated Texas has allowed private employers to opt out since 1913. Employers that do are non-subscribers: they lose the protection of statutory liability limits and can be sued by injured employees. In 2024, 24% of Texas private employers were non-subscribers, and they employed 13% of the state's workers, the lowest share in a decade. Workers' comp insurance rates in Texas have fallen 81% since 2003.

Ratemaking. Rates are set per $100 of payroll. Ratemaking service organizations collect accident data and publish rating manuals with rates by business classification. Many employers are then experience rated, so a bad claims history costs more.

Second-injury funds. Some workers arrive with a disability from a previous job. These funds let employers hire them without fearing liability for the earlier injury.

The insurance market, in one paragraph.Updated NCCI's 2026 report shows private carriers posted a 91% combined ratio in 2025 (they paid out 91 cents in losses and expenses for every premium dollar), the 12th consecutive year of underwriting profit. Net written premium slipped 0.2% to $41.6 billion, and approved NCCI loss cost filings were expected to lower written premiums by about 5% from 2025 to 2026. But lost-time claim frequency fell only 2%, while claim severity rose about 4% for both medical and indemnity. Fewer claims, each more expensive: the trend to watch.

Claims and the four kinds of benefits

Employees file with the state commission that administers the program. There are three kinds of claims. An injury claim covers a disability from a workplace accident, including repetitive strain. An occupational disease claim covers an ailment tied to a trade or process (silicosis, asbestosis, radiation illness). A death claim covers a death in the course of employment or from a compensable injury or disease. Which injuries and illnesses count varies by state.

Unlimited medical care

Usually paid without limit on amount or time, though state fee schedules cap what is paid for particular procedures.

Disability income

Four types by duration and extent (see the tool below). Permanent partial disability is either scheduled (loss of a specific body part) or nonscheduled (general injuries such as back or head damage).

Death benefits

A burial allowance plus survivors' benefits for the spouse and dependent children. Amounts and durations vary widely by state, and spousal benefits often end after a set number of weeks or on remarriage.

Rehabilitative services

Physical and vocational rehabilitation, available in all states. Claims must usually be made within six months to two years, and in most states the employer pays.

Employer rights: immunity, and its four exceptions

Complying with workers' comp protects the employer from lawsuits based on the no-fault principle. That immunity has four exceptions:

Intentional acts

Deliberate and knowing torts (intent to harm), or violating an affirmative duty, such as failing to tell workers about exposure to a harmful substance.

Retaliation

In most states an employee may sue for demotion, pay cuts, or firing after filing a claim. The employer carries the burden of proving a legitimate business necessity.

Noncompliance

An employer that skips the required coverage can lose immunity and face fines, criminal penalties, or liability for the full claim. Most states still pay the injured worker.

Dual capacity

When the employer plays a second role toward the employee, such as manufacturer of a product that injures him, common-law suits may survive.

Employers can defend by showing the harm was unrelated to work: a preexisting condition (unless work made it worse), employee negligence or misconduct, or safety violations. Even so, comp payments generally continue if an employee is fired for violating work rules.

Part Six: After the Pandemic

What became of the COVID-19 rules

The textbook asks whether serious COVID-19 illness is a compensable workers' comp claim and answers that it depends on the state. It describes laws in California, Illinois, New Jersey, Utah, Wisconsin, and Wyoming, and executive orders in Connecticut, Kentucky, and New Hampshire. That was accurate for 2020–2021. It is now history.Updated

The presumptions ended. Texas's COVID-19 presumption for first responders stopped applying to injuries on or after June 15, 2023, when the state disaster declaration expired. California's presumptions, including the "outbreak" rules the book describes, were repealed effective January 1, 2024.

Ordinary rules apply again. An employee who contracts COVID-19 today must generally prove it is more likely than not that the exposure was work-related. The book's warning that pinning down where a person caught the illness is difficult remains the whole problem.

Presumptions live on elsewhere. The lasting legacy is the expansion of presumption laws in general. At least 34 states now provide some coverage for mental-only injuries, and a growing number have PTSD presumptions for first responders.

The lesson for this course: emergency rules are written for the emergency. When the emergency ends, benefits law tends to snap back to its baseline, and the baseline (state by state, employer by employer) is what an HR professional actually administers.
