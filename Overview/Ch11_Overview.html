Chapter 11: Contingent Workers and Remote Work Arrangements

Not everyone who does a company's work is its employee. Who counts as an employee, who is responsible for each worker's benefits, and what changes when the workplace is a living room are legal questions with real money attached.

Big idea: Benefits follow legal status. Whether a worker is an employee of the company, of a temp agency, of a lease company, or of no one, the label decides who owes workers' compensation, retirement-plan access, and health-plan obligations. So the central skill is not memorizing four categories. It is asking who controls the work, who employs the worker for which purpose, and what happens if the label turns out to be wrong.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

The textbook's Chapter 11 runs on 2020–2021 data and law. Its March 2020 benefits access figures, its report that a court struck down California's Proposition 22, and its three-year test for long-term part-time employees have all been overtaken: the California Supreme Court upheld Proposition 22 in 2024, SECURE 2.0 shortened the part-time test to two years, BLS ran its first contingent-worker survey since 2017, and the Department of Labor is rewriting the federal contractor test. Remote work has also settled from a pandemic necessity into a stable share of the workforce.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (BLS, the Department of Labor, court decisions, and federal statutes) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker; where the textbook is wrong, you'll see Corrected. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: Who counts as contingent?

Workers without a promise of ongoing work

An employee expects long-term or indefinite employment. A contingent worker has no implicit or explicit contract for ongoing employment. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) treats the difference as a matter of expectation, and the textbook sorts contingent workers into four groups:

Part-time employees

Fewer than 35 hours a week, by choice or not.

Temporary and on-call employees

Fill in for absent employees or cover busy periods.

Leased employees

Placed by a lease company in a client firm, usually for a year or more.

Independent contractors, freelancers, and consultants

Set up working relationships on their own.

Two BLS measures, not one. BLS measures contingent work (a job the person does not expect to last) separately from alternative arrangements (independent contractor, on-call, temporary help agency, or contract firm). They overlap only partly: of 16.3 million workers in alternative arrangements, 1.6 million (9.8%) were also contingent. A long-term independent contractor is in an alternative arrangement but not contingent.

Part Two: Part-time employees

Part-time work: legally an employee, practically a savings

The BLS defines a part-time worker as someone who works fewer than 35 hours a week. A voluntary part-time employee chooses that schedule. An involuntary part-time employee works fewer than 35 hours because full-time work is unavailable. Companies that employ part-time workers are their legal employers, exactly as they are for full-time employees. Whether they award discretionary benefits is what suggests the worker's status.

Noneconomic reasons, such as school or family, explain nearly 80% of part-time work in the textbook's data, and I did not find a newer national figure for that share. Most employers do not provide discretionary benefits to part-time workers, though practices vary widely. Where part-timers do get paid time off, the annual cost is lower because they get fewer days or none.

The legal floor: three rules that reach part-timers

The Affordable Care Act. An employer is not required to offer health coverage to workers under about 130 hours a month. A part-time worker who does receive health insurance is entitled to COBRA continuation coverage.

Retirement plans. Employers may have to include part-time employees who are at least 21 and complete 1,000 hours in a 12-month period.

The SECURE Acts. Congress added a path for long-term part-time employees: those who work at least 500 but fewer than 1,000 hours in each of several consecutive years.

Corrected The two-year rule. The textbook says the SECURE Act requires three consecutive years of 500 hours. Since plan years beginning in 2025, SECURE 2.0 requires only two consecutive 12-month periods. The employee must be at least 21, and the plan must allow elective deferrals (no employer contribution is required). Periods beginning before 2023 do not count toward the two-year test, and the rule now also reaches 403(b) plans covered by ERISA. Hours in each qualifying year count toward vesting if the plan makes employer contributions.

Part Three: Temporary, on-call, and leased workers

Who is the employer when a third party is involved?

Temporary and on-call workers fill in for employees on approved leave and cover busy periods. A temporary employment agency is the most common source; it is the workers' legal employer for most purposes, screens them, pays them, evaluates their performance, and trains them. Employers have two other routes: direct hire arrangements, which typically keep the worker under 1,000 hours over 12 months (below the ERISA threshold for qualified retirement plan participation), and on-call arrangements, in which the hiring company employs the worker when called and takes responsibility for HR, including benefits.

Leased employees are placed with a client by a lease company under a contract that typically runs a year or longer. The lease company bills the client the direct costs of employing the worker plus a fixed fee, either a fixed percentage of the client's payroll or a fixed amount per employee. The law treats the lease company and the client as co-employers, which affects who provides benefits.

A lease company can also make sense for reasons beyond labor cost: it takes over W-2 forms, IRS reporting, payroll administration, and unemployment insurance administration, and it can pool many employers' workers to negotiate better benefits. The SECURE Act extended this idea to retirement with pooled employer plans, which let smaller employers join one plan to negotiate lower fees.

Part Four: Independent contractors

Employee or contractor? The label is a legal conclusion

Independent contractors, freelancers, and consultants set up working relationships with companies on their own, not through an agency or lease company. They usually have specialized skills in short supply, and they take gigs when they choose to. Phone apps now link contractors to customers; drivers for Uber, or deliverers for DoorDash and Instacart, are the familiar examples.

The stakes are high because companies owe nothing in workers' compensation premiums (unless a state requires it) and are not bound by ERISA, the FMLA, the NLRA, Title VII, the ADEA, the ADA, GINA, the ACA, or the SECURE Act for genuine contractors. That is why the test for status matters. Courts and agencies use three: the common-law test, the economic realities test, and a hybrid.

Common-law test (control)

  • Developed by judges, case by case
  • Asks whether the employer has the right to control the worker
  • The IRS sorts the evidence into behavioral, financial, and type-of-relationship categories

Economic realities test

  • Applied mainly under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)
  • Asks whether the worker is financially dependent on the company for work
  • A worker who depends on someone else's business for continued work is classified as an employee

What changed since the textbook

Updated The federal test keeps moving. A 2024 Department of Labor rule used a six-factor, totality-of-the-circumstances test. In May 2025 the DOL stopped enforcing it (Field Assistance Bulletin 2025-1), and on February 26, 2026 it proposed replacing it with a five-factor test that weights two "core" factors most: the degree of control and the worker's opportunity for profit or loss. The proposal would also apply to FMLA coverage. Comments closed April 28, 2026. I did not find a final rule, so check dol.gov before relying on it.

Updated California's Proposition 22. The textbook reports an August 2021 ruling striking it down. On July 25, 2024, the California Supreme Court unanimously upheld it in Castellanos v. State of California. App-based rideshare and delivery drivers may be classified as independent contractors, but companies must provide benefits such as a health care subsidy and occupational accident insurance.

State law can be stricter than federal law. States such as California, Massachusetts, and New Jersey apply their own, tougher tests, so a worker who passes the federal test may still be an employee under state law.

Part Five: Benefits for each group

Who owes what

Whatever the label, every company that uses contingent workers has some liability under federal and state law: overtime and minimum wage under the FLSA, workers' compensation premiums, and nondiscriminatory benefits practices under ERISA, the NLRA, Title VII, the ADA, the ADEA, GINA, the ACA, and the SECURE Act. Temporary agencies and lease companies are liable under these laws, but the host or client can be legally responsible too.

Why the label is a temptation. The average gap in benefit cost between a full-time and a part-time hour is about $12 (June 2026: $17.03 versus $5.05). A company that saves that by choosing a label carries the risk that the label is wrong: back pay, unpaid payroll taxes, and benefits owed retroactively.

Part Six: Remote work

Working from home: same law, new questions

Remote work and telecommuting are nearly the same. The term remote work gained currency as a pandemic necessity; telecommuting is a negotiated flexible arrangement in which employees work at home or another location. The potential benefits for employers are higher productivity, lower overhead for office space and supplies, and help with recruiting and retention. The concerns are the loss of direct contact, which makes performance appraisal harder, and employees who feel that remote work disrupts their lives or isolates them.

Benefits for a remote workforce

Most companies offer the same benefits regardless of work location, and legally required benefits stay in force. Companies may emphasize benefits that help remote workers manage stress: telemedicine that includes counseling, financial education, and employee assistance programs. The unsettled question is workers' compensation, which was built around injuries on company premises.

How companies limit their risk

Write a telecommuting policy that states the employer's expectations for people who work from home.

Set home office guidelines: a designated work area and training on workstation setup, safety, and ergonomics.

Where possible, conduct periodic checks of home offices. The instructor's materials note that privacy laws and health concerns can make this impractical.

Set fixed work hours and meal and rest periods for telecommuters, so it is clear when an injury happened during work.

These steps sound straightforward, but the reality is not: workers' compensation laws vary by state, courts rule differently, and legal advice is often needed.

Critical thinking

Three ways to add 40 workers: which one is really cheapest?

The situation. A 220-employee regional grocery distributor needs 40 more workers for its warehouse and delivery routes. The VP of operations has three proposals: (A) hire 40 part-timers at 28 hours a week with no benefits, (B) contract the work to a staffing agency, or (C) engage the 15 delivery drivers as independent contractors using their own vehicles. She wants whichever is cheapest. Ten office employees are also asking to work remotely three days a week.

  1. For each of the three options, who is the legal employer and which obligations stay with your company?
  2. Identify at least two legal risks across the options and say how you would reduce each.
  3. What information would you gather before deciding, and what would show that the "cheapest" option is not cheap?

Your response stays in your browser. It is not saved or submitted. Copy it into your course platform when you are done.

There is no single right answer. A strong response takes a position and shows it has weighed these points:

  • Option A: part-timers. The company is the legal employer. Watch the ACA's 130-hours-a-month line (28 hours a week is about 121 a month, but hours drift), COBRA for any who get health coverage, and SECURE 2.0: employees who reach 500 hours in each of two consecutive years and are 21 or older must be allowed to defer into the 401(k). Hours must be tracked, which is a cost.
  • Option B: a staffing agency. The agency is the legal employer for most laws, but workers' compensation depends on the dual employer doctrine and the contract, and the host can still be legally responsible. The agency's markup replaces the benefits savings. Ask what the contract says about workers' compensation and who directs the work.
  • Option C: contractors. The riskiest. Drivers who follow company routes and schedules, are paid by the hour, drive under the company's name, and do work that is central to the business look like employees under the common-law and economic-realities tests. The federal test is in flux (a 2024 rule, then non-enforcement, then a 2026 proposal), and state tests may be stricter. Misclassification can mean back pay, unpaid payroll taxes, and retroactive benefits.
  • Count the true cost. The national average benefits gap is about $12 an hour, but the full cost includes turnover, training, supervision, agency markup, legal exposure, and the risk that part-time and contract workers are less committed. Compare total cost per productive hour, not the wage.
  • Remote work. The same benefits and laws apply. A telecommuting policy, home-office guidelines, fixed work hours, and clear meal and rest periods reduce workers' compensation exposure. Ask whether the ten office employees' jobs actually need on-site presence.
  • A test and a stop rule. Try option A with one route or one shift first, and track hours, turnover, injuries, and cost per hour worked. Decide in advance what result, such as turnover above the current rate, would make you switch.
