Chapter 5: Employer-Sponsored Health-Care Plans

A roadmap through the benefit that costs employers the most, confuses employees the most, and changes the fastest.

Big idea: Every health plan design in this chapter buys cost control by taking something away from the patient — choice of provider, freedom from paperwork, or dollars out of pocket. Learn to spot which one is being traded, and the whole chapter organizes itself.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

Your textbook's health-care chapter runs on 2020–2022 figures, and almost none of them are still accurate. Premiums, deductibles, HSA and FSA limits, ACA penalty amounts, and the legal status of several provisions have all changed — in some cases dramatically.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, IRS, HHS, and KFF) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom of this page. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: The Big Picture

What is a health-care plan?

A health-care plan covers the cost of services that support physical and mental health: exams, diagnostic testing, surgery, hospitalization, psychotherapy, dental work, corrective lenses. Simple enough. The interesting question is who stands behind the promise to pay.

Fully insured plans

  • Employer contracts with an insurance company
  • The insurer assumes the risk of paying claims
  • The insurer administers the plan
  • Employer pays a negotiated premium
  • Subject to state insurance regulation

Self-funded plans

  • Employer decides what to cover
  • The employer pays claims from its own assets and assumes the risk
  • No premiums paid in advance, so cash stays with the employer
  • Makes sense when expected claims cost less than premiums
  • Largely exempt from state insurance mandates under ERISA

That last line on each side is why self-funding has quietly taken over. 67% of covered workers were in a self-funded plan in 2025, including 80% at large firms.Updated When you hear that a state passed a law requiring coverage of some treatment, remember it generally does not reach the self-funded plans where most covered workers actually are.

Three more distinctions worth keeping straight

Individual vs. group coverage. Individual coverage is bought outside the employment setting. Extending coverage to most or all employees is group coverage — the basis of everything in this chapter. Group plans generally cannot exclude a member based on health status; the health of the group sets the employer's premium instead.

Single vs. family coverage. Single covers only the employee; family adds qualified dependents. The cost gap between them is enormous, and it falls mostly on the employee.

Multiple-payer vs. single-payer. The U.S. is a multiple-payer system: government, employers, employees, and individuals all pay parts of the bill. Canada is the standard contrast — a single-payer system funded through taxation.

How insurers set the premium. Underwriting uses mortality tables (annual probability of death), morbidity tables (annual probability of illness), and experience ratings (this group's actual claims history). A workforce with more hospitalizations and surgeries pays more next year. This is exactly why a company with a healthy, young workforce may find self-funding attractive — and why the sickest groups have the least bargaining power.

Where employer coverage came from

Nothing about employer-sponsored health insurance was inevitable. It exists because of a series of accidents.

  • Late 1800s. Mining and railroad companies hire doctors to treat workers. The first company medicine.
  • 1930s. The Depression produces the Social Security Act of 1935 — but President Roosevelt keeps health coverage out of it. Nonprofit Blue Cross forms; for-profit insurers follow with fee-for-service plans.
  • World War II. Wartime wage freezes leave employers unable to compete on pay, so they compete on benefits instead. Health coverage becomes a recruiting tool. This is the hinge of the whole story.
  • 1960s. Medicare and Medicaid arrive as Titles XVIII and XIX of the amended Social Security Act. Demand surges past supply; costs inflate.
  • 1970s. ERISA (1974) protects participants' interests; the HMO Act of 1973 pushes employers toward managed care with financial incentives.
  • 2010. The ACA converts health coverage from a discretionary benefit into a legal requirement for large employers.

Why this history still matters. Coverage is tied to your job for no medical reason whatsoever — it's a leftover from a 1940s wage-control workaround. Every hard question in American health policy (job lock, coverage gaps between jobs, why your plan changes when you change employers) traces back to that accident.

Who pays the premium

Among private industry workers in medical plans, employers pay 80% of the single-coverage premium and 69% of the family premium. Employees pick up the rest — 20% and 31% respectively. In dollars, that averaged $1,440 per year for single coverage and $6,850 for family coverage in 2025.Updated

The employer share is not distributed evenly. For family coverage, workers in the lowest 10% of wages pay 41% of the premium themselves; workers in the highest 10% pay 27%. The employees least able to afford coverage are charged the largest share of it.

The cost trend in one comparison. Family premiums rose 6% in the most recent year, against general inflation of 2.7% and wage growth of 4%. Over five years, family premiums are up 26%. Health care does not merely get more expensive — it outruns both prices and paychecks, year after year, which is the pressure behind every plan-design change in the rest of this chapter.

Three kinds of medical expense benefits

Hospitalization benefits

Inpatient covers overnight stays: room and board plus related services such as nursing and meals. Outpatient covers treatment without an overnight stay — emergency room care, preadmission testing, same-day surgery.

Surgical benefits

Pay for medically necessary procedures, not elective ones like cosmetic surgery. Paid on a schedule of usual, customary, and reasonable charges; anything above that level is the patient's to pay.

Physician benefits

Cover physician fees tied to hospital stays or office visits. In extenuating circumstances — where travel would endanger the patient — a plan may cover home visits.

Part Two: The Rules

Federal regulation of health plans

HMO Act of 1973

Promoted HMOs with federal incentives. Its dual choice requirement once forced employers with at least 25 employees to offer an HMO alongside a fee-for-service plan. Dual choice was eliminated in 1995, but a related protection survives: an employer may not financially penalize an employee for choosing the HMO option, and must contribute the same amount toward it as toward other plans.

ERISA (1974)

Covers pension and welfare plans, which include medical, surgical, and hospital benefits. Four parts of Title I matter here: reporting and disclosure, fiduciary responsibilities, continuation coverage, and group health plan standards for portability, access, and renewability. COBRA (1985) and HIPAA (1996) are ERISA amendments you met in Chapter 3. So is the Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act of 1998, which requires plans covering mastectomies to also cover reconstruction for symmetrical appearance.

ERISA also preempts most state regulation of self-funded plans. That single word explains why a state mandate can cover your neighbor's plan and not yours.

ADA (1990)

Prohibits discrimination based on disability, enforced by the EEOC. But Congress recognized that benefit plans rest on risk assessment, so the ADA permits disability-based distinctions in benefit design where they are grounded in sound actuarial principles.

Your book lists the 2021 penalties as $2,700 and $4,060. They are now $3,340 and $5,010 — roughly 24% higher. "Pay or play" is a real calculation for employers, and the price of "pay" keeps climbing.

Where the legal fights landed. Congress zeroed out the individual mandate penalty effective 2019. In 2021 the Supreme Court disposed of California v. Texas on standing — the challengers could not show injury from a $0 penalty — leaving the ACA intact without resolving the underlying question.

The most recent challenge was decided June 27, 2025. In Kennedy v. Braidwood Management, the Court held 6–3 that the requirement to cover preventive services recommended by the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force without cost-sharing is constitutional. Task Force members are properly appointed and answer to the HHS Secretary, who may remove them and review their recommendations. Practical effect for your future employees: screenings and preventive medications stay free at the point of care.

Still prohibited under the ACA: preexisting condition exclusions, and lifetime or annual dollar limits on essential health benefits. The book is right about those.

State regulation

Every state regulates insurers' practices, generally reaching fully insured plans but not self-funded ones. State laws address four areas: which services and conditions must be covered, reimbursement of recognized providers, who must be covered, and how long coverage continues after employment ends. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) coordinates across states.

Part Three: Plan Design Alternatives

Four questions that sort every plan

The Bureau of Labor Statistics distinguishes plan types with four questions. Learn these and you can classify a plan you have never seen before — including the one on your own future benefits enrollment form.

  • Is the plan an indemnity plan (reimburses provider or patient) or a prepaid plan (pays providers a fixed amount per covered person)?
  • Does the plan have a network?
  • Does it allow nonemergency care outside the network?
  • Does it have more than two levels of coverage?

Fee-for-service plans

Protection against health expenses paid as cash to the employee or directly to the provider after services are rendered — a reimbursement basis. Three categories of eligible expense: hospital, surgical, physician. Participants may use any licensed provider. No network at all. Maximum freedom, minimum cost control. This is why these plans have nearly vanished.

Managed care plans

Managed care controls cost by limiting the choice of doctors and hospitals. Three common forms:

HMO

Prepaid: a fixed periodic fee covers members, but only for services delivered by network providers and approved by the HMO. Requires a primary care physician as gatekeeper. An open-access HMO adds coverage for out-of-network emergency care.

PPO

A selected group of providers agrees to serve a population at reduced reimbursement. Providers qualify by meeting quality standards, following cost-containment procedures, and accepting the PPO's reimbursement structure. In exchange, the employer steers patients to them with financial incentives.

EPO

A stricter PPO. Reimburses only for services inside the network, but does not require a primary care physician.

POS

Combines fee-for-service and HMO features. Nearly identical to a PPO except that it requires a primary care physician. Nominal copayments in network; out-of-network care available at higher cost.

Types of HMO — how the doctors are organized

Staff model. The HMO owns the facilities and employs physicians on salary. Those physicians treat only HMO members. Specialists may be contracted under a capped fee schedule, with anything above the cap passed to the patient.

Group model. Contracts with established multi-specialty practices rather than employing physicians directly. Pays through a preset fee schedule or a monthly amount per patient.

Network model. Like the group model, but contracts with two or more independent practices, compensated on a capped fee schedule.

Individual practice associations (IPAs). Partnerships of independent physicians and practices that charge lower fees to a designated population. These physicians see both HMO enrollees and non-enrollees.

What the market actually looks like now. Among private industry workers in medical plans in 2024, 75% had two plan networks available and 18% had one. Nonemergency out-of-network services were available to 78% of participants — meaning for the other 22%, out-of-network care is entirely their own expense. PPOs remain the most common plan type overall.Updated

Features shared across plans

Whatever the plan type, the same cost-sharing machinery appears: deductible, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum, preadmission certification, and second surgical opinions. Two features in your textbook's list are now illegal under the ACA: preexisting condition clauses, and lifetime or yearly dollar limits on essential benefits.

Deductible. What you pay before the plan pays anything. May vary by whether care is in network, and some employers scale it to salary. The average single-coverage deductible reached $1,886 in 2025 — $2,631 at small firms versus $1,670 at large ones.Updated

Coinsurance. The percentage of covered expenses you pay after the deductible. The classic split is 80/20: the plan pays 80%, you pay 20%.

Out-of-pocket maximum. The annual ceiling on your cost-sharing, protecting against catastrophic expense. In 2025, 72% of covered workers faced a single-coverage maximum above $3,000, and one in five above $6,000.

Preadmission certification. A nurse or physician employed by the insurer must approve nonemergency hospitalization in advance, and determines an appropriate length of stay. Unauthorized admissions may not be paid.

Second surgical opinions. Reduce unnecessary procedures by having another physician weigh in; the plan covers the consultation. Some insurers require it, others merely offer it.

Part Four: Consumer-Driven Health Care

Shifting the first dollars to the employee

Managed care was supposed to control costs. It did not control them enough. So employers turned to a different lever: raise the deductible sharply, hand the employee a tax-advantaged account, and let the employee feel the price of care directly. That is a consumer-driven health-care plan (CDHP) — a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) paired with a pretax account.

The three-tier structure

A CDHP is often described as a three-tier payment system. Tier 1: the employee spends pretax account money. Tier 2: the coverage gap — the difference between the account balance and the deductible, paid out of pocket. Tier 3: the plan pays expenses above the deductible.

The FSA risk runs both ways. Employees who overestimate their expenses forfeit the difference. But employers carry risk too: under the uniform coverage requirement (risk-of-loss rule), the full elected annual amount must be available to the employee from day one of the plan year, no matter how little has been contributed. An employee who elects $3,400, spends it in January, and quits in February leaves the employer holding the balance.

New in 2026 — the HSA rules just expanded.Updated The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 4, 2025) made three changes your textbook cannot contain, clarified by IRS Notice 2026-05:

· The telehealth safe harbor is now permanent. An HDHP may cover telehealth and remote care before the deductible without destroying HSA eligibility — retroactive to plan years beginning on or after January 1, 2025. The pandemic-era workaround your book describes as temporary is now law. The safe harbor does not extend to in-person services or drugs prescribed during a virtual visit.

· From January 1, 2026, bronze and catastrophic exchange plans count as HSA-compatible even if they don't meet the general HDHP definition.

· Certain direct primary care arrangements no longer disqualify a person from contributing to an HSA, and the periodic membership fee is now an HSA-eligible expense.

Part Five: The Rest of the Package

Other health-related benefits

Dental

Covers routine preventive procedures and work necessary to the health of teeth and gums; cosmetic improvement generally excluded. Three delivery forms: dental fee-for-service (usual, customary, and reasonable charges, with deductibles, coinsurance, and lifetime maximums), dental service corporations (nonprofits owned by state dental associations, prepaid with 20–30% copayments), and dental maintenance organizations or dental HMOs (prepaid, approved providers, nominal copayments).

Access tracks employer size closely: 30% of private industry workers at firms under 100 employees, 50% at 100–499, and 70% at 500 or more.

Vision

Typically covers exams, lenses, frames, and fitting, delivered through indemnity or managed care arrangements, with limits on frequency and exclusions for specialty lenses, sunglasses, and contacts not medically necessary. Access by firm size: 21% / 34% / 44% across the same three size bands.

Prescription drugs

Medical reimbursement plans

Reimburse some or all of the drug cost, usually tied to self-funded or indemnity plans. Deductibles apply, coinsurance is typically 80%. Most expensive of the three, because pharmacies charge full retail and administration costs are high.

Prescription card programs

Prepaid with nominal copayments at participating pharmacies, often built on formularies — lists of drugs judged clinically appropriate and cost effective. Less expensive because insurers negotiate volume pricing.

Mail order programs

Dispense maintenance medications for chronic conditions such as hypertension or high cholesterol. Usually paired with a card program.

Multiple tiers

Copayments scale by drug category: generic lowest, formulary brand-name higher, non-formulary brand-name highest. The intent is to steer patients toward cheaper equivalents. Research shows it also causes some patients to stop taking their medication altogether — a cost saving that isn't one.

Mental health and substance use

Plans cover psychiatric medication, psychological testing, inpatient and outpatient care, with benefit amounts often varying by diagnosis under the DSM-5. Employee assistance programs (EAPs) serve as the entry point and help employers address problems interfering with work.

The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act of 2008 requires that a group health plan offering mental health and substance use benefits alongside medical and surgical coverage treat them equally in out-of-pocket cost, benefit limits, prior authorization, and utilization review.

Parity enforcement is in flux.Updated Federal agencies issued a strengthened MHPAEA rule in September 2024. After an employer trade group sued, the Departments of Labor, HHS, and Treasury announced on May 15, 2025 that they will not enforce the new portions of that 2024 rule while the litigation is resolved, plus 18 months afterward. The underlying statute, the 2013 regulation, and the requirement to prepare comparative analyses of nonquantitative treatment limitations all remain in force — and state regulators are not bound by the federal non-enforcement policy. A good illustration for this course: a rule on the books is not the same as a rule being enforced.

Maternity care

No federal law requires employers to offer maternity benefits, but several shape them. The Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978 forbids treating pregnancy less favorably than other medical conditions. The FMLA (1993) entitles eligible employees at companies with 50 or more employees to 12 unpaid weeks for the birth of a child. The Newborns' and Mothers' Health Protection Act of 1996 sets minimum hospital stay lengths and bars financial incentives to shorten them. Some states mandate maternity benefits for all but self-funded plans.

Telemedicine

Telemedicine uses electronic and telecommunication technology to deliver care when patient and clinician are not in the same place. It expanded enormously during the pandemic and did not retreat: it removes exposure risk, reaches rural patients, and serves people with mobility limits. Two concerns persist — unequal access to devices and broadband, and HIPAA privacy risk in transmitting patient data. As noted above, the HSA-compatibility question that once complicated telehealth in HDHPs has now been settled permanently in its favor.

Retiree health-care benefits

Employers face a strong financial disincentive to cover retirees, for two reasons. The first is obvious: medical costs rose. The second is an accounting change most students never think about.

FAS 106 (1990). Changed how companies recognize the cost of non-pension retirement benefits. Those costs must be carried as an expense, which reduces reported net profit. The FASB's reasoning: post-retirement benefits are part of the compensation exchange, so they belong on the books.

FAS 132 (2003). Requires substantial disclosure about the economic value and cost of retiree health programs, including plan asset categories, allocation decisions, and concentrations of risk.

FAS 158 (2005). Pushed transparency further for other post-retirement employee benefits.

The pattern to take away: an accounting rule changed a benefit. Nothing about retirees' medical needs changed in 1990. What changed was that the obligation became visible on the balance sheet, and companies without the assets to sustain it began withdrawing the offer.
