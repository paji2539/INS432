Chapter 8: Paid Time-Off Benefits

The benefit with the fewest federal rules and, increasingly, the most state rules. Federal law protects your job while you are away. Who pays you while you are gone is mostly left to the employer, and now to the states.

Big idea: Sort every kind of time off with two questions. Is it paid or unpaid? Is it discretionary or mandated? Most paid leave is discretionary. Most mandated leave is unpaid. The gap between those two facts is where state paid-leave laws, and most of the legal risk in this chapter, live.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

The textbook's Chapter 8 runs on March 2020 access data and September 2020 cost data. Its state paid-leave examples (Massachusetts at $850 a week, New York at $840.70) are years out of date, and its count of federal holidays has changed.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Department of Labor, OPM, and state-program data compiled by New America) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: The Big Picture

Paid time off: what it is and who gets it

Paid time-off policies compensate employees when they are not performing their primary duties. Private employers offer most of them on a discretionary basis. In nonunion workplaces many, holidays especially, exist as a matter of custom. In union workplaces they are written into the collective bargaining agreement. The one large exception is unpaid family leave, which federal law guarantees. Employees value paid time off for rest, leisure, and family obligations. Employers value it for recruiting, retention, and the chance to "recharge" a workforce. It is part of the psychological contract you met in Chapter 2.

That is the point of the book's remark that paid leave costs are directly linked to wages: a paid day off is valued at what the worker would have earned, so wage growth lifts the cost automatically. It also explains why the highest costs sit in the best-paid occupations even though a manager and a clerk may get the same number of days.

Part Two: Everyday Leave

Holidays, vacation, sick leave, and the PTO bank

Holidays

Private employers generally follow the federal government's lead. Federal law now names 11 legal public holidays, after Juneteenth (June 19) was added in 2021.Updated The book counts 12, apparently because Inauguration Day is also a legal holiday, but it applies only to federal employees in the Washington, D.C., area, and only every fourth year. When a holiday falls on a Saturday or Sunday, federal employees usually get the nearest weekday off: in 2026 the Fourth of July is observed on Friday, July 3. Many private employers add one or two floating holidays, so employees can observe any day that is not on the employer's list.

Holidays can be changed by the stroke of a pen, but only for those who work for the government. In December 2025 an executive order gave federal employees December 24 and 26 off. It applied to 2025 only and did not add permanent holidays. The lesson for HR: a private employer follows the federal calendar by custom, not by law, and custom can be revised.

Religious holidays need care. Christmas is the only religious holiday on the federal list, but Title VII prohibits religious discrimination. EEOC guidelines require employers to allow employees to observe a religious day unless it causes undue hardship, and the observance of religious holidays must be treated uniformly as paid or unpaid time off.

Vacation: five design choices

Eligibility. Employees usually qualify after six months to a year of full-time work. Part-timers may be excluded or paid in proportion to their hours.

Single or multiple policies. Separate policies usually give managers, professionals, and executives more time, in recognition of their greater effect on results.

How much time. A fixed number of days, days that grow with seniority (most common), or hours-based accrual for nonexempt workers under the FLSA.

Scheduling conflicts. Approval requirements, with seniority breaking ties when two employees want the same days.

Unused time. Three answers. Carryover lets you use it later. Use it or lose it forfeits it at year's end. A cash-out pays the daily value of the unused days. Federal law does not require private employers to pay for accrued, unused vacation, but some states require it when employment ends.

Sick leave

Sick leave pays for a set number of days of absence for personal illness, and it is separate from disability leave (Chapter 6). Employers that offer it commonly grant five to nine days, and some require evidence of illness. The tension is real. Unscheduled absences disrupt work, and illness is the most socially acceptable reason for them. But workers who cannot afford unpaid time, or fear being fired, come to work sick, and then healthy coworkers get ill.

State and local governments are now setting minimums. The textbook's examples: Nevada requires 0.01923 hour of paid leave for each hour worked, and Colorado requires at least 1 hour of paid sick leave for every 30 hours worked, up to 48 hours a year. Federal contractors may fall under Executive Order 13706 (2015), which requires up to seven days of paid sick leave for covered employees. DOL's fact sheet on the order was last issued in January 2025. Check the current status before relying on it for any contract you manage.Updated The practical rule for HR: find out every jurisdiction where you have employees, and apply the most generous rule.

The paid time-off bank

An integrated paid time-off policy, or PTO bank, folds holidays, vacation, sick days, and personal days into one pool that employees use without giving a reason. The pitch is that it reduces unscheduled absences, since nobody has to fake an illness to take a day, and it eliminates the administrative burden of tracking reasons and processing medical certifications. Good designs share three features:

  • Slow growth. For example, one day a month in the first year (up to 12 days), rising to about 16 days in years two through five.
  • Carryover instead of use-it-or-lose-it, so there is no flood of absences in December.
  • Carve-outs. Bereavement, jury duty, military leave, and sabbaticals stay separate, because they are driven by events outside the employee's control, by law, or by long service.

The downside gets less attention. A single bank can cost the conscientious employee: someone who used to keep five sick days in reserve now spends the pool on a long weekend, and then has nothing left when she is really ill.

Bereavement leave

Bereavement or funeral leave gives paid time off after a death, usually a relative's, and often scales by relationship: longer for a parent or spouse, shorter for a cousin or friend. No federal law requires it, which is why the leave sorter below sends a grandparent's funeral to "employer policy."

Part Three: The FMLA

Job protection, not pay

Before the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993, most workers who needed extended time for a serious illness or a new child had to quit. The FMLA gives eligible employees up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period. It does not require pay. It does require the employer to maintain group health coverage on the same terms as if the employee were working. Employees may substitute paid vacation or personal leave for unpaid FMLA leave in certain circumstances. The Department of Labor's current fact sheets show the core rules unchanged.Updated

Which employers are covered

  • Private-sector employers with 50 or more employees in 20 or more workweeks of the current or previous calendar year
  • Public agencies (federal, state, and local) regardless of size
  • Schools (public and private elementary and secondary) regardless of size

Which employees are eligible

  • At least 12 months with the employer (not necessarily consecutive)
  • At least 1,250 hours of service in the 12 months before leave starts
  • Works where the employer has at least 50 employees within 75 miles

Reasons for leave

  • The birth and care of a newborn, or placement for adoption or foster care.
  • Care for a spouse, child, or parent with a serious health condition.
  • The employee's own serious health condition.
  • Two military-related forms added later: qualifying exigency leave (up to 12 weeks for families of National Guard and Reserve members called to active duty) and military caregiver leave (up to 26 weeks in a single 12-month period to care for a service member with a serious injury or illness).

Employees generally give 30 days' notice when the need is foreseeable. Leave may be taken all at once or intermittently. Spouses who work for the same employer share a combined 12 weeks for birth, adoption, or a parent's care. Since March 27, 2015, employees in legal same-sex marriages have the same rights to care for a spouse.

Coming back

On return, the employee must be restored to the original job or an equivalent one with equal pay, benefits, and terms, and keeps benefits earned before leave. There is one narrow exception: an employer may refuse to restore the highest-paid 10 percent of employees if reinstatement would cause substantial economic injury, and only if it notifies the employee of ineligibility, tells the employee as soon as it decides, offers a reasonable chance to return, and makes a final decision at the end of leave if the employee asks to return.

What the FMLA does not do. It doesn't pay anyone. Where paid family leave exists, it comes from the employer or, in a growing number of states, from a state program (California's began paying in 2004). A worker at a 30-person company, or one with 1,100 hours instead of 1,250, or one at a small satellite office, has no FMLA rights at all. The tool above lets you find out where that line sits.

Part Four: State Paid Family and Medical Leave

Where the pay comes from now

There is still no federal paid family and medical leave program. States have filled the gap.Updated As of May 2026, 12 states plus D.C. pay benefits: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Maine, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, and Washington. Maryland begins paying in January 2028 after several delays, and Virginia, which enacted its program in April 2026, begins in December 2028. The book's Massachusetts example is a good measure of how far the programs have moved: it describes an $850 weekly maximum, and the 2026 maximum is $1,230.39.

Leave criteria. Every program covers bonding with a new child, family caregiving, and the worker's own serious health condition. Some add military exigency and "safe" leave for survivors of domestic violence. Massachusetts allows up to 12 weeks of family leave and 20 weeks of medical leave, capped at 26 weeks a year in total.

Pay amount. The book describes flat percentages. Most newer programs use a sliding scale that replaces a higher share of low wages, because a flat 60% is not enough for someone who lives paycheck to paycheck. California's benefit rose to 90% for lower earners in January 2025.

Funding. Small, mandatory payroll contributions from employers, employees, or both, pooled in a state fund. No state's rate exceeds 1.3% in 2026, and most are 1% or less. New York uses private insurers alongside a state fund, and Delaware has employers administer the program with state reimbursement.

Part Five: Leave the Law Shapes

Jury duty, witness duty, and military service

Jury duty

The Jury Systems Improvement Act of 1978 makes federal jury service a protected right: employers may not prohibit it or treat those who serve less favorably. State laws are similar. Most employers pay nonexempt employees while they serve. The FLSA bars employers from docking exempt employees' salaries for jury service, apart from narrow exceptions (offsetting the court's payment for a service of less than a workweek, or when no work is performed for a week or more).

Witness duty

Governed by state law, and the textbook counts about 20 states plus Puerto Rico and D.C. Statutes usually define who counts (a crime victim, or someone under subpoena), prohibit retaliation, require reasonable notice, and set penalties.

Military leave: USERRA

The Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act of 1994 protects reemployment for voluntary or involuntary service, if cumulative service with the employer does not exceed five years. It bars discrimination in hiring, retention, promotion, and benefits. Employees may continue employer health coverage for up to 24 months, and the employer must restore benefits they would have earned, such as seniority.

Military families: FMLA

Two FMLA provisions cover families: qualifying exigency leave when a Guard or Reserve member is called up, and military caregiver leave of up to 26 weeks for a seriously injured service member.

Part Six: What Counts as Work Time

Nonproduction time, on-call time, and extended leave

Nonproduction time

  • Time related to the job but not the job itself (cleanup, preparation, travel between sites) or nonwork periods within a shift (rest and meal breaks)
  • The FLSA requires pay for principal activities and for activities integral and indispensable to them
  • The Supreme Court held that Amazon warehouse security screenings were not compensable (Integrity Staffing Solutions v. Busk). The California Supreme Court reached the opposite result for mandatory exit searches at Apple stores (Frlekin v. Apple), reasoning that the employer controlled employees during the searches.

On-call time

  • Time on or near the premises, where the employee cannot use it for personal purposes
  • Employers can require nonexempt employees to be available and must pay at least the FLSA minimum wage for that time
  • Exempt employees may be required to be on call with no extra pay
  • Courts look at how much the on-call duty restricts personal life. Wearing a beeper was not enough in Owens v. Local 169

The lesson of the two cases is that the same fact can be compensable in one jurisdiction and not in another. Multistate employers should not assume that a federal ruling ends the question.

Sabbaticals

Sabbatical leave is common in universities, where faculty who meet service requirements (usually three years of full-time work) receive partial or full pay for up to an academic year. Other employers use sabbaticals for rejuvenation and development. The textbook says about 18% of companies offer one, roughly half paid. Employers typically require a minimum period of employment after return, or repayment of part of the pay. Five types exist:

Traditional sabbaticals to renew or retool work skills.

Personal growth leaves for education or new competencies.

Social service leaves for significant public service, which also builds the company's image.

Extended personal leaves: unpaid leave of two or more years with a return-to-work guarantee, often for caregiving.

Voluntary leaves to meet business needs: extended unpaid time off during downsizing or slack demand.

Paid time off to volunteer

Volunteerism leave arrangements vary widely (some employers allow up to two weeks a year, or add a donation to the employee's charity). Employers offer it for five reasons: work-life balance, corporate social responsibility and public image, skills-based volunteering as professional development, the "helper's high" that lowers stress, and retention.

Critical thinking

One pool of days, two very different states

The situation. A distribution company has 120 employees: 70 in Massachusetts and 50 in Texas. It offers 12 vacation days, 5 sick days, and 2 personal days, plus paid holidays. HR sees a spike of unscheduled absences on Mondays and Fridays and a rush of vacation requests every December. The HR director proposes replacing the three policies with a single PTO bank of 19 days, with carryover of up to 5 days, and telling employees they do not need to give a reason. The CFO likes the proposal because it will simplify administration, and asks what could go wrong.

  1. Who is better off and who is worse off under the bank? Name at least one employee type in each group.
  2. Two employees, one in Massachusetts and one in Texas, each need eight weeks off for surgery in March, having already used their bank on a January trip. What does each one get? Is that fair, and what, if anything, should the company do?
  3. What evidence would you collect before deciding, and what result would make you reject the proposal?

Your response stays in your browser. It is not saved or submitted. Copy it into your course platform when you are done.

There is no single right answer. A strong response takes a position and shows it has weighed these points:

  • Winners and losers. Employees who rarely use sick days gain flexibility. Frequently ill employees, and those with chronic conditions or young children, may lose the protected sick days they had and burn the bank early. Consolidation can also shrink total time off if the new bank is smaller than the sum of the old policies.
  • The bank does not change the law. The Massachusetts employee can draw on state paid medical leave (up to 20 weeks, at up to $1,230.39 a week, before any employer top-up) and is covered by the FMLA if eligible. The Texas employee has only the FMLA's unpaid protection, if eligible, plus whatever the employer chooses to provide. The same policy therefore produces a very different outcome by state.
  • Compliance floor. Any single bank has to meet the strictest state sick-leave and accrual rule in each place the company has employees. A design that works in Texas may not satisfy a mandated-sick-leave state.
  • Fairness and retention. The company could add a small separate medical-leave reserve, allow advance borrowing, or top up state benefits for long absences. Each costs money and sets precedent. A strong answer prices at least one of them.
  • Evidence. The absenteeism pattern itself needs diagnosis. Are Monday and Friday absences illness, burnout, or scheduling? Useful data: absence by day and department, pre-policy usage of each leave type, how many employees exhaust sick days, December request volume, and exit interviews. Reject or redesign the proposal if the data show that the current problem is workload or scheduling rather than the policy structure.
  • The textbook's claim to test. The book says integrated banks control unscheduled absenteeism better than separate policies. Ask what evidence supports that, and whether any of it is specific to your workforce.
