Chapter 10: Managing the Employee-Benefits System

Choosing what to offer is only half of benefits management. The other half is who gets to choose, how employees learn what they have, how to keep the bill from running away, and who does the administrative work.

Big idea: Every design decision in this chapter trades three things against each other: choice (does each employee get what he or she needs?), cost (who pays, and how much?), and complexity (how hard is it to run, explain, and keep legal?). A cafeteria plan buys choice with complexity. Cost-control tools buy lower spending by shifting risk or friction onto employees or providers. For every tool, ask what it trades away, and who bears it.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

The textbook's Chapter 10 runs on pre-2021 data and tax limits. Health-plan costs, flexible spending and savings account limits, and the way employers deliver required plan documents have all changed. A 2025 tax law raised the dependent care limit and widened HSA eligibility, and in July 2026 the Department of Labor proposed letting health plans deliver many required notices electronically by default. The textbook's statement on how often an SPD must be refreshed also does not match the regulation.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (KFF, the IRS, the Department of Labor, and federal regulations) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker; where the textbook is wrong, you'll see Corrected. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: Fixed or flexible?

One package for everyone, or a menu?

A traditional benefits plan gives every employee the same set of benefits at the same level. It is simple to run and simple to explain, and it is how most employers began: the employer picks a package, largely on cost, and everyone gets it. Its weakness is fit. A 26-year-old with no dependents and a 55-year-old with tuition bills and an aging parent value the same package very differently.

A cafeteria (flexible) benefits plan lets employees choose among designated benefits and levels of benefits. Companies increasingly replace fixed plans with flexible ones to meet the needs of a diverse workforce and, they hope, to see a return through recruiting and retention.

Traditional (fixed) plan

  • Same benefits for every employee
  • Administrative ease: one package to price, explain, and audit
  • Cheap to run, but poor fit when employees' needs differ

Cafeteria (flexible) plan

  • Employees choose benefits and levels within limits the employer sets
  • Aims to raise the value employees see in every benefit dollar
  • Costs more to administer, communicate, and keep legal

The textbook reports limited evidence that employees react well to flexible plans: higher satisfaction with benefits, with the job overall, and with pay, and a better understanding of their benefits after the plan is introduced. The word to notice is limited. It is a reason to expect a payoff, not proof of one.

Beyond the textbook: adverse selection. When employees choose, those who expect high medical bills tend to pick the richest coverage and healthy employees pick less. If too many healthy people leave the richest option, its price rises, which pushes out even more of them. Employers manage the risk with pricing rules, employer credits, and limits on what can be chosen.

Part Two: The tax rule behind cafeteria plans

Section 125: the price of choice

Internal Revenue Code Section 125 lets participants pay for certain benefits with pretax dollars, but only if the plan follows a strict rule: participants must be able to choose between at least one taxable benefit (such as cash) and at least one qualified benefit. A plan that offers a choice only among taxable benefits is not a Section 125 plan. A qualified benefit is an employer-sponsored benefit whose cost the employee may exclude from federal income tax.

Allowable qualified benefits (textbook Exhibit 10.3)

  • Employer-provided accident and health plan
  • Group term life insurance
  • Health savings accounts
  • Dependent care assistance
  • Adoption assistance

Prohibited (textbook Exhibit 10.3)

  • Medical savings accounts (Archer MSAs)
  • Scholarships
  • Educational assistance programs
  • Fringe benefits such as employer-sponsored transportation services

The textbook's exhibit is a summary. Whether a specific benefit can run through a cafeteria plan turns on the Code and the plan document, so employers check with counsel before adding one.

Guidelines every Section 125 plan must follow

In writing. The plan must be a written document.

A real choice. Employees choose between two or more benefits, one nontaxable and one taxable.

Current and former employees only may participate.

Yearly elections made before the plan year begins, changeable mid-year only for qualifying life events such as the birth of a child.

No deferral of pay from year to year. A benefit that defers an employee's receipt of compensation is prohibited.

Nondiscrimination. The plan cannot give preferential treatment to highly compensated participants and key employees.

Three ways to violate the nondiscrimination rules

Eligibility

The plan must include at least some non-key employees.

Benefits

All employees must get the same employer contribution, the same eligibility rules, and the same benefits available.

Concentration

The value of nontaxable benefits provided to key employees cannot exceed 25% of the total nontaxable benefits under the plan.

Which group pays for a violation depends on the violation. Failing the tests eliminates the tax benefits for one or both groups: highly compensated or key employees, or everyone else. A concentration failure costs the key employees, who lose the exclusion.

Updated Who counts as "key" or "highly compensated." Chapter 12 defines both. For 2026 an officer is a key employee at pay above $235,000 (up from $230,000 in 2025), and a highly compensated employee is anyone whose prior-year pay was above $160,000. A 5% owner is always both.

Part Three: Four ways to build a flexible plan

Salary reduction, modular, core-plus, and mix-and-match

Employers use four common designs. They differ mainly in how much choice the employee has and who carries the cost of that choice.

An illustration (not from the textbook). An employer allots an employee 8,000 credits. She buys single medical coverage (5,400), dental (500), long-term disability (450), and five extra vacation days (900). That uses 7,250 credits and leaves 750, which she can spend on vision coverage (150) or, if the plan allows, take as taxable cash.

Flexible spending accounts and the 2026 limits

Before each plan year, employees elect the amount of salary reduction to send to an FSA. FSAs usually cover expenses beyond the plan's regular limits. Elections are made up front and the account is use-it-or-lose-it: a plan may offer either a limited carryover or a short grace period, not both.

Part Four: Making sure employees understand what they have

Communicating the benefits program

Benefits communication is not a courtesy; it is half the value of the program. Two sets of considerations shape it: what the law requires, and what makes good business sense.

What the law requires: ERISA disclosure

The Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) requires employers to give employees written notices, in language the average participant can understand. Two documents carry most of the load.

Summary plan description (SPD)

  • Who administers the plan, with names and addresses
  • A description and explanation of the benefits
  • Employees' rights under ERISA
  • Eligibility criteria and conditions for disqualification or suspension
  • Claims procedures, including how to appeal a denial
  • Whether the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation insures the retirement plan

Summary of material modifications (SMM)

  • Describes important changes: plan administrators, claims procedures, eligibility rules, vesting
  • Due within 210 days after the end of the plan year in which the change occurred
  • Goes to employees and the Department of Labor

Timing (textbook): the SPD goes to employees and the Department of Labor within 120 days of a plan becoming subject to ERISA's reporting rules, and to each new participant within 90 days of joining.

Corrected How often must the SPD be refreshed? The textbook says an updated SPD is due every fifth plan year even when nothing changed. The Department of Labor's regulation says five years if the plan was amended, ten years if it was not, each measured to 210 days after the end of the relevant plan year.

Updated Delivering documents electronically. Since 2020, retirement plans have had a "notice-and-access" safe harbor that makes electronic delivery the default. On July 23, 2026, the Department of Labor proposed a similar safe harbor for group health plans. Today, health plans must generally rely on a 2002 rule that permits electronic delivery only to employees who are "wired at work" or who consent. The proposal is not final, and comments were due September 21, 2026.

What makes good business sense

An effective communication program has four objectives: to promote recruitment and retention; to help employees understand what is available so they choose wisely at open enrollment; to build awareness and appreciation of how benefits improve employees' financial security and physical and mental well-being; and to tell employees what costs the employer covers to make benefits affordable. The textbook's slides and its discussion questions say "three" objectives but list four. Learn the four.

Candidates read the careers section of a company's website and social media to learn what benefits it offers. New hires get group meetings during onboarding, where the key message is that some elections, such as medical plans, are locked in until the next enrollment. Communication then continues for three reasons: reminders keep choices in mind and raise use; employees decide each year whether to keep or change elections; and an annual benefits statement shows the value of benefits and each side's share of the cost.

Open enrollment and benefits choice

Open enrollment is the period when employees may change, elect, or drop benefits. It usually runs two weeks to a month, at the same time each year, a month or two before the benefits plan year, the 12-month period elections stay in effect. A plan year can follow the calendar year or the fiscal year.

Helping employees choose well

Poorly informed choices lead to unused benefits and a belief that benefits are not worth much. Employers offer online and printed materials, benefits counselors, live seminars, and sometimes access to a financial adviser. Even so, many employees ignore the resources.

Larger employers are increasingly using artificial intelligence to recommend a plan. The tool asks questions, sometimes including income, and compares the answers with data from many earlier employees. It can point two employees with the same income toward the same high-deductible plan even though their debts differ. It can also raise legal problems: saving answers about health conditions in a database may violate privacy laws such as HIPAA or equal-employment laws such as GINA.

Part Five: Keeping the bill under control

Managing the costs of employee benefits

Benefits costs are high and keep rising, so employers use eight families of tools. Almost all of them work the same way: they shift some cost, risk, or friction from the employer onto employees or providers.

Employee contributions and waiting periods

Pretax contributions let employees exclude the amount from income before federal and state income tax. After-tax contributions do not reduce taxable income.

Waiting periods set a minimum time before an employee becomes eligible and often match the probationary period. The Affordable Care Act limits waiting periods for health coverage to 90 days.

High-deductible plans and employee education

A high-deductible plan shifts spending from the employer to the employee through a larger annual deductible, and it lowers premiums immediately. In 2026 a plan qualifies for an HSA only if its deductible is at least $1,700 (single) or $3,400 (family). Some employers use the same idea for workers' compensation: a much lower premium in exchange for agreeing to pay claims up to a deductible.

Education matters because overuse has causes. Tax breaks made employer coverage feel standard, and older fee-for-service plans let people see costly specialists freely. Teaching employees what care costs, together with higher contributions, is meant to reduce unnecessary use.

Utilization reviews and case management

Prospective (precertification)

Before treatment: verifies coverage, checks whether the plan covers it, and judges medical appropriateness. May require a second surgical opinion.

Concurrent

During a hospital stay: judges whether more inpatient days are medically necessary, often the day before discharge.

Retrospective

After care but before the insurer pays: confirms coverage and that the treatment was given and appropriate, which also screens for fraud.

Case management companies help participants with serious injuries or illnesses, whether acute (possibly life threatening) or chronic (ongoing), get essential care cost-effectively. Nurses or social workers work with physicians to balance medical need and cost.

Provider payment systems

Managed care plans negotiate how they pay doctors, hospitals, and pharmacies. The four arrangements differ sharply in how well they control total cost.

How managed care plans pay providers
SystemHow it worksDoes it control total cost?
Percentage discountReduces providers' usual charges by a percentageWeakly. There is no limit on the number of services, and providers can raise their regular fees.
Capped fee scheduleSets a maximum dollar amount for each service, usually a percentage of usual, customary, and reasonable feesWeakly. It caps price but not volume.
Partial capitationPays a primary care physician a fixed monthly amount for each assigned patientBetter. Costs are easier to predict because pay follows the number of patients.
Full capitationPays a fixed amount per assigned patient, and holds the physician accountable for the cost of servicesStrongly. Total outlay does not rise with volume.

The catch with capitation. Because the physician keeps what is not spent, full capitation creates a financial incentive not to authorize treatments and specialist referrals. It controls cost by design, and that is also the reason it worries patients and regulators.

Lifestyle interventions

A lifestyle intervention is any activity that changes how a person lives, such as quitting smoking. Most wellness programs are voluntary. Some employers go further. Scotts Miracle-Gro adopted a policy of terminating employees who would not quit smoking on or off the job; a new hire, Scott Rodrigues, was fired after a drug test found nicotine, and the judge ruled against his ERISA claim on all counts. The ruling does not reach states that prohibit firing workers for off-duty smoking. Chapter 9 covers the legal limits on wellness incentives.

Part Six: Who does the work?

Outsourcing the benefits function

Outsourcing is a contract under which an employer transfers responsibility for a function to a third-party provider: an independent company with expertise in benefits design and administration. The textbook reports that companies outsource on average about two-fifths of their benefits functions, and that the top reason is expertise. Administration of employee assistance programs, flexible spending accounts, and COBRA continuation coverage are among the most commonly outsourced services.

Outsourcing can be expensive at first because most or all fees are due up front. Over time, an efficient arrangement can cost less than employing several people to administer benefits, and it frees the company to spend its own effort on work that cannot be outsourced well, such as product design.

A gap in the data. The two-fifths figure is the textbook's and I did not find a current national statistic to replace it. Treat it as a rough benchmark and ask what share of your employer's functions would be worth outsourcing, and why.

Critical thinking

Choice or chaos? A menu for 400 employees

The situation. A 400-employee regional distributor offers one fixed benefits package. About 40% of employees are under 35, about a quarter are 55 or older, and the mix is hourly and salaried. The CFO worries that a flexible plan will raise costs. Employees say they do not understand what they already have. The vendor is pitching a mix-and-match plan with an AI enrollment assistant that asks about income, family, and health to recommend a plan. Two people in HR would administer it.

  1. Which flexible design would you recommend, if any (salary reduction only, modular, core-plus-option, or mix-and-match), and what are you trading away by choosing it?
  2. Identify at least two legal or design risks in the vendor's proposal, and say how you would reduce each.
  3. Say how you would measure whether it worked after two open enrollments, and what result would make you go back to the fixed plan.

Your response stays in your browser. It is not saved or submitted. Copy it into your course platform when you are done.

There is no single right answer. A strong response takes a position and shows it has weighed these points:

  • Match the design to capacity. With two HR staff, a simpler design (salary reduction plus a small core-plus-option menu) may beat full mix-and-match. The trade is less fit for less administrative and communication burden. A strong answer names the trade.
  • Adverse selection. If employees choose freely, healthier workers may leave the richest medical option and raise its price. Pricing rules, employer credits, and limits on what can be chosen are the usual controls.
  • Nondiscrimination. Any Section 125 plan must pass the eligibility, benefits, and 25% concentration tests, and the employer must know who its key and highly compensated employees are. A plan design that lets a few highly paid employees take most of the nontaxable benefits fails.
  • Privacy. The textbook warns that saving specific health answers in a database may violate HIPAA or GINA. A strong answer limits what the tool asks for, keeps health data out of employment decisions, and reviews the vendor's data handling in writing.
  • Do employees understand it? The problem the employees report is understanding. Choice without understanding produces underused benefits and a perception of little value. Test comprehension, such as whether employees can say what their plan covers, before and after.
  • A real measure and a stop rule. Pick measures in advance: satisfaction and understanding, enrollment errors and mid-year change requests, turnover among new hires, cost trend by plan, and administrative hours. State the result that would send you back, for example administrative hours rising past capacity without a gain in understanding.
  • Pilot first. Roll out to one site or one employee group and compare before committing all 400.
