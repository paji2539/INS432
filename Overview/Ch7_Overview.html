Chapter 7: Government-Mandated Social Security Programs

Four programs from one 1935 law, and a financing problem that the textbook calls a projection and the Trustees now give a date.

Big idea: Social Security is not a savings account. It is a pay-as-you-go promise: today's payroll taxes pay today's beneficiaries. Nearly every rule in this chapter is an answer to one of two questions. Who is paying whom, right now? And what happens when the money coming in does not cover the money going out? Learn to ask those two questions and the chapter organizes itself.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

The textbook's Chapter 7 runs on 2020–2021 figures, and this is the chapter where that hurts most. Trust fund dates, benefit amounts, Medicare premiums, the taxable wage base, and the whole Medicare Part D drug benefit have changed. A 2025 law rewrote the rules for some public employees, and the pandemic unemployment programs the book describes are gone.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (SSA, CMS, the Social Security and Medicare Trustees, the U.S. Department of Labor, and KFF) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: The Big Picture

A Depression-era law, still running

The government created these programs to promote the social good: a strong economy, low unemployment, decent wages and benefits, and safe workplaces. The Great Depression showed what happens without them. Long spells of unemployment caused income discontinuity and pushed families toward destitution. Protecting worker income also protected the economy, and kept some companies in business.

  • 1935. The Social Security Act sets up two programs: a federal system of retirement income and a system of unemployment insurance run jointly by the federal government and the states.
  • 1939. Amendments add survivors' insurance.
  • 1965. Amendments add disability insurance and Medicare, providing health protection for people 65 and older.
  • Nine-digit numbers. The Act also authorized a wage-recordkeeping system, which produced the Social Security number in the late 1930s. SSA issues numbers to citizens, resident aliens, and foreign students.

OASDI

Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance: retirement income, income for the survivors of deceased workers, and income for disabled workers and their families. "Old-age" simply means retirement.

Medicare

Health insurance for nearly all citizens 65 and older, and for disabled Social Security beneficiaries.

Unemployment insurance

Weekly income for workers who lose jobs through no fault of their own. Run by the states within federal rules.

Supplemental Security Income

A cash program for aged, blind, and disabled people with little income, funded from general tax revenue, not payroll taxes. The book sets it aside as unrelated to employee benefits. The 2026 federal payment is $994 a month for an individual.

Who must participate, and who runs it

Exempt groups (OASDI and Medicare). Federal civilian and railroad employees with 10 years of service are exempt from the retirement program but not from Medicare. State and local government employees already covered by another retirement plan are exempt from Social Security contributions unless the organization opts in. Children under 21 are exempt, except those 18 and older working in a parent's business.

OASDI and Medicare administrators. The Social Security Administration runs retirement, survivors, and disability insurance, and SSI. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services runs Medicare.

Unemployment insurance administrators. Titles III and IX of the Act let the federal government grant money to states to administer benefits and created a federal trust fund. The Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA) governs the payroll tax: the federal share pays administration costs and the state share may be used only for benefits. The Department of Labor's Employment and Training Administration oversees the program federally. Each state agency keeps records, collects taxes, decides eligibility, and pays claims.

New since the book: WEP and GPO are gone.Updated Many state and local government employees, including teachers, firefighters, and police officers in many states, never paid Social Security tax and earned a pension instead. Two rules, the Windfall Elimination Provision and the Government Pension Offset, cut the Social Security benefits they or their spouses could collect. The Social Security Fairness Act, signed January 5, 2025, repealed both, retroactive to January 2024. SSA said the two rules had reduced benefits for more than 2.8 million people, and by July 2025 it had sent more than 3.1 million payments totaling $17 billion. If a public-sector worker in your family looks at the textbook's exemptions, the exemption still exists. The penalty attached to it does not.

Part Two: OASDI

Retirement, survivors, and disability

Qualifying: the credit system

Eligibility runs on Social Security credits, also called quarters of coverage. You earn one credit for a set amount of covered earnings in a calendar quarter, up to four credits a year. You need 40 credits (about ten years of work) to qualify for retirement benefits, and you never lose credits when you change jobs or become unemployed. Anyone born in 1929 or later needs 40 credits; once you have them you are fully insured for life.

How the benefit is calculated

SSA pays set monthly benefits. Three ideas do the work:

AIME (average indexed monthly earnings) summarizes your indexed lifetime earnings before age 62, disability, or death.

PIA (primary insurance amount) is the monthly benefit at full retirement age, or paid to a disabled worker. It comes from your AIME through a progressive formula: it replaces a larger share of a low earner's past pay than of a high earner's. The textbook says the formula gives every beneficiary about the same proportion of income. It does not.

COLA (cost-of-living adjustment) is applied each January to protect against inflation. It follows the rise in the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners (CPI-W) from the third quarter of one year to the next. The 2027 COLA will be announced in October 2026.

Retirement: when you claim matters

Full retirement age (FRA) depends on your birth year. The phase-in from 65 to 67 that the book describes as ending in 2022 is finished: anyone born in 1960 or later has an FRA of 67.Updated You can claim as early as 62, but the benefit is permanently reduced, by up to 30% when FRA is 67. Waiting beyond FRA earns delayed retirement credits of 8% a year until age 70. Family benefits generally equal 50% of the worker's PIA.

Survivor benefits

A worker generally needs 40 credits to qualify family members. Eligible survivors include:

  • A nondisabled widow(er) at full retirement age, or a disabled widow(er) as early as 50
  • A widow(er) of any age caring for the worker's children
  • A divorced spouse as early as 60
  • Unmarried children under 18 (19 if in high school full-time), and disabled children of any age if the disability began before 22
  • Stepchildren, grandchildren, or adopted children in most cases, and dependent parents 62 or older

Monthly survivor benefits range from 71.5% to 100% of the PIA. A lump-sum payment goes to a surviving spouse or child who meets the requirements.

Disability benefits

SSA pays only for total disability: inability to engage in substantial gainful activity (SGA) because of a medically determinable impairment expected to result in death or to last at least 12 months. The worker must pass two tests: the recent work test, which considers age at disability, and the duration of work test, which asks whether you worked and paid in long enough. The benefit equals the full PIA, and family members usually get half. Payments begin after a five-month waiting period.

SGA in 2026.Updated Earning more than $1,690 a month generally counts as substantial gainful activity ($2,830 for people who are statutorily blind). A trial work period month is any month with earnings over $1,210. These thresholds rise each year with wages, so a paper that cites the book's numbers is out of date.

Compare this program with workers' compensation from Chapter 6. Workers' comp pays for work-related injury with a waiting period of days. Social Security pays for long-term, total disability from any cause, after five months.

Part Three: Medicare

Five pieces, two ways to enroll

Medicare serves nearly everyone 65 or older who has 40 credits, automatically covering spouses. It also covers people with a disability who have received Social Security disability benefits for 24 months, and people with permanent kidney failure. Approximately 99% of beneficiaries pay no Part A premium because they have at least 40 quarters of covered work.Updated

Part A: hospital

Inpatient hospital care, skilled nursing facility care (up to 100 days), home health, and hospice. The 2026 inpatient deductible is $1,736 per benefit period.

Part B: medical

Voluntary. Doctors' services, outpatient care, lab work, some preventive services, and ambulance transport. After a $283 annual deductible, the plan pays 80% of approved amounts.

Medigap

Voluntary private insurance that fills gaps in Parts A and B, such as deductibles and coinsurance. Plans are standardized by letter. A subset called Medicare Select lowers premiums by limiting your choice of providers.

Part C: Medicare Advantage

Private plans that replace Original Medicare: HMOs, PPOs, special needs plans, and medical savings account plans (see Chapter 5). Created as Medicare+Choice in 1997, renamed in 2004.

Part D: drugs

Prescription drug coverage through private plans, first offered in 2006 under the Medicare Modernization Act. Completely redesigned in 2025 (see below).

Original Medicare or Medicare Advantage?

Original Medicare

  • Fee-for-service, run by the federal government
  • Use virtually any licensed provider or facility
  • No out-of-pocket maximum unless you add supplemental coverage such as a Medigap plan

Medicare Advantage

  • Run by private companies under strict Medicare rules
  • Managed care networks (HMOs, PPOs), with extra benefits such as dental and vision
  • Includes an annual out-of-pocket limit, which Original Medicare lacks

55% of eligible Medicare beneficiaries, 35.2 million of 64.2 million, were in a Medicare Advantage plan in 2026, up from 19% in 2007. Enrollment growth has slowed, and nearly a quarter of Advantage enrollees are now in special needs plans.Updated Managed care, the tool employers used in Chapter 5, has become the majority choice in Medicare too.

Part D: from four stages to three

The textbook walks through four stages: deductible, initial coverage, coverage gap, and catastrophic. Under the Inflation Reduction Act, the coverage gap ("donut hole") disappeared on January 1, 2025.Updated In 2026 the standard benefit is:

  • Deductible: up to $615. Some plans have less or none.
  • Initial coverage: you pay 25% of the cost of covered drugs.
  • Catastrophic: once your out-of-pocket spending hits $2,100, you pay $0 for covered drugs for the rest of the year.

Part Four: Paying for It

FICA, the wage base, and the running-out date

OASDI and Medicare are funded through equal employer and employee contributions under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA). Self-employed people pay the whole thing under the Self-Employment Contributions Act (SECA). The biggest slice funds OASDI, and OASDI tax applies only up to a taxable wage base. The Medicare (hospital insurance) tax has no cap.

The running-out dates

Social Security is a pay-as-you-go system. Money deposited today pays today's benefits, and nothing guarantees that the same benefits will be there for today's workers. The Trustees report each year on how long the reserves will last. The textbook quotes the 2020 report. Here is the 2026 report, released June 9, 2026.

What "depletion" does and does not mean. When a trust fund's reserves hit zero, benefits do not stop. They are limited to what continuing payroll tax income can pay, which the Trustees estimate at about 78% of scheduled OASI benefits. For a retiree at the 2026 average benefit of $2,071 a month, a 22% cut would be roughly $450 a month. The 2026 report moved the OASI date one quarter earlier than the 2025 report.

The textbook offers three pressures behind these numbers: people live longer, immigrant workers' lower wages produce less tax, and unemployment leaves millions not contributing. The demographic pressure is the one that has not gone away.

Part Five: Unemployment Insurance

A federal-state partnership

Unemployment insurance (UI) provides weekly income to workers unemployed through no fault of their own. Each state runs its own program within federal rules, and the states' tax collections go into trust funds held by the federal government. Being unemployed does not by itself qualify you. Generally you must:

Have lost your job for an acceptable reason. Quitting usually disqualifies you unless you had reasonable cause, meaning working conditions no sensible person could tolerate. Being fired does not guarantee eligibility either. Misconduct, refusing suitable work, certain labor disputes, and misrepresentation disqualify you.

Meet earnings and employment minimums during a base period. States use one of four methods: high-quarter (HQ), multiple-quarter (MQ), annual-wage (AW), or weekly-wage (WW).

Serve a waiting period. Most states impose a one-week wait after filing.

Be able and available to work (capacity is ability, availability is motivation) and actively seek suitable work. Suitable work uses skills similar to your customary work and does not violate labor laws.

How much, and for how long

Most states pay 50% to 67% of prior earnings, within a minimum and a maximum. Benefits usually run up to 26 weeks. But "usually" hides a wide spread, as the Department of Labor's January 2026 summary of state laws shows.Updated

The pandemic programs are gone

The book describes the CARES Act's $600 weekly supplement, longer duration, and coverage of contingent workers, then the $300 weekly supplement extended through September 6, 2021. All of those programs ended on September 6, 2021, and no federal supplement is active today.Updated As of the Department of Labor's September 17, 2026 report, about 1.58 million people were collecting regular state benefits (an insured unemployment rate of 1.0%, down from 1.2% a year earlier), and no state had triggered the Extended Benefits program that adds weeks when state unemployment is high.

Who pays: FUTA and SUTA

FUTA (federal)

  • Employer tax of 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages
  • Employers who pay state tax on time get a credit of up to 5.4%, so the effective rate is 0.6%, or $42 per employee
  • The federal share pays administration costs

SUTA (state)

  • Each state sets its own wage base (at least $7,000) and rates
  • Rates are experience rated: an employer that lays off many workers pays more than one that lays off few
  • Paid only by employers in nearly all states. The state share may be used only for benefits

When a state can't pay, its employers pay more.Updated A state that borrows from the federal government and cannot repay loses part of the FUTA credit for its employers. For 2025, California had a 1.2% credit reduction (raising its effective FUTA rate to 1.8%, or up to $126 per employee) and the Virgin Islands had 4.5%. Connecticut and New York repaid in time and avoided it. State maximum SUTA rates for experience-rated employers now run as high as 14.37% in Massachusetts, compared with the book's 2021 high of 12% in Wisconsin.
