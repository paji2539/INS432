Chapter 12: Nonqualified Deferred Compensation Plans for Executives

Executives are paid in ways the rest of the workforce is not, because the tax code's limits and fairness rules for ordinary retirement plans do not fit their pay. The trade: more money set aside, in exchange for more risk, more scrutiny, and one very unforgiving tax law.

Big idea: Every feature of executive compensation answers one question: who bears the risk? An unfunded promise leaves the executive exposed to the company's creditors. A stock option puts the executive at the mercy of the share price. A clawback or a parachute moves risk between the board, the executive, and shareholders. When you meet a plan, ask who is exposed if things go wrong, and who is watching.

⚠ About the numbers on this page

The textbook's Chapter 12 runs on 2021 tax limits and several outdated descriptions. Its retirement-plan pay limits ($290,000, $230,000, and $58,000) are now $360,000, $290,000, and $72,000, and its $185,000 officer threshold is now $235,000. More important, its description of how stock options and phantom stock are taxed is wrong in ways that matter, it never mentions Section 409A (the tax law that governs nearly every deferral plan), and the SEC's clawback rule now forces public companies to recover pay after certain restatements, whether or not the executive did anything wrong. The SEC has also proposed reducing pay disclosure for most public companies.

Every statistic and dollar amount on this page has been updated from primary sources (the IRS, the SEC, court and agency actions, and published pay studies) and is cited in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure has moved far enough to matter, you'll see an Updated marker; where the textbook is wrong, you'll see Corrected. Use these numbers, not the book's, on quizzes and papers.

Part One: Who is an executive?

Two tax categories: key employees and highly compensated employees

The Internal Revenue Service recognizes two groups of employees who play a central role in a company's competitive strategy. It uses key employee to decide whether a qualified retirement plan needs top-heavy provisions, and highly compensated employee for the nondiscrimination rules that govern employer-sponsored benefits.

Key employee (any time during the year)

  • An officer paid more than $235,000 (2026)
  • A 5% owner
  • A 1% owner paid more than $150,000, a threshold set by statute and not indexed
  • Officer means an administrative executive in regular and continued service. A title without authority is not an officer; authority without a title is. A partner is not treated as an officer merely because of a partnership interest.

Highly compensated employee

  • A 5% owner at any time during the year or the preceding year
  • Or, for the preceding year, an employee paid more than $160,000
  • Or, if the employer chooses, one of the top-paid group: the top 20% most highly compensated employees

Nonqualified deferred compensation: the basic idea

A nonqualified deferred compensation (NQDC) plan is an agreement between an employer and an employee to pay compensation in the future. NQDC plans are the hallmark of executive pay. Companies use them for one of two objectives:

Restoration

Fills the gap between the maximum retirement benefit the IRS allows a qualified plan and the larger benefit that a very high salary would otherwise earn.

Supplemental benefits

Goes beyond restoration to raise total retirement benefits well above what a restoration plan would pay. Supplemental executive retirement plans (SERPs) do this.

Part Two: Why these plans are "nonqualified"

Three ways nonqualified plans differ from qualified ones

Nonqualified plans differ from qualified plans in three ways: ERISA qualification criteria, funding status, and mandatory retirement age.

1. They fail at least one ERISA criterion

Plans that meet all of ERISA's minimum standards are qualified and get favorable tax treatment. Executive plans fail at least one, usually for three reasons:

Pay above the limits. Executives' pay often exceeds the Code's limits, so confining them to qualified plans would leave much of their compensation uncovered.

Coverage. Executive plans will invariably fail ERISA's coverage requirements, which prohibit favoring highly compensated employees disproportionately.

Nondiscrimination. Executive plans violate rules that bar discrimination in favor of highly compensated employees in contributions, benefits, availability, rights, or plan features.

2. Funded or unfunded

Funded plan

  • Places money or property (for example, company stock) in a trust or buys insurance contracts in the executive's name, without risk of forfeiture
  • Secures the promised payments
  • Neither the company nor its creditors can withdraw the money

Unfunded plan

  • Contains only a promise to pay in the future
  • No designated funds stand behind the promise
  • The executive is an unsecured creditor if the company fails

Three factors push companies toward funding: managing liabilities (funding forces resources to be set aside), cost (funding is a significant expense, so it must promote longer-term returns such as profit or reputation), and shareholder sensitivity (shareholders may view funding an executive plan as a detrimental cost during public debate about excessive pay).

3. Mandatory retirement age

Normal retirement age is the lowest age at which an employee who retires may begin receiving benefits. The Age Discrimination in Employment Act forbids mandatory retirement for virtually all employees. The exception, per the textbook, covers an employee 65 or older who, for the two years before retiring, was a bona fide executive (primarily manages the company and directs two or more people) or in a high policymaking position (plays a significant role in policy but lacks direct line control), and who is entitled to an immediate nonforfeitable annual retirement benefit of at least $44,000.

Corrected The tax law the textbook leaves out: Section 409A. Since 2004, Internal Revenue Code Section 409A has governed almost every deferral arrangement, including SERPs, deferral plans, executive employment and severance agreements, and many equity awards. The main rules: an election to defer generally must be made before the year in which the pay is earned (30 days after first becoming eligible, or six months before the end of a performance period of at least 12 months); payment may occur only on six events (separation from service, disability, death, a change in control, an unforeseeable emergency, or a fixed date); and certain "specified employees" of public companies must wait six months after leaving. The penalty for violating it falls on the executive: income tax on all vested amounts at once, a 20% additional tax, and interest.

Part Three: The two main executive retirement plans

Excess benefit plans and SERPs

Excess benefit plan

  • Increases retirement benefits by the amount lost to IRS limits
  • Designed as an extension of a qualified defined benefit or defined contribution plan
  • May be funded or unfunded. A funded plan is subject to ERISA's reporting, disclosure, fiduciary, and enforcement rules; an unfunded plan is not
  • Vesting schedules tend to be shorter

Supplemental executive retirement plan (SERP)

  • Provides benefits well above restoration
  • Can count more than base pay, such as bonuses and incentive pay
  • Can be a succession-planning tool: full benefits at an earlier age or with fewer years of service
  • Can use richer formulas (for example, the highest three years of salary instead of career average) and offset provisions
  • Vesting schedules tend to be longer, which promotes retention

The top hat plan

A well-known unfunded SERP is the top hat plan, maintained "primarily for the purpose of providing deferred compensation for a select group of management or highly compensated employees." By design, it is exempt from three parts of ERISA's Title I: minimum standards for participation and vesting, funding, and fiduciary responsibilities. Practitioners coined the term; no statute or regulation defines it. To keep the exemption, the plan must stay unfunded and limited to a select group, but ERISA defines neither unfunded nor select group. Companies rely on Department of Labor advisory opinions and on often-conflicting court rulings.

Part Four: How the promise is backed

Funding mechanisms: from least to most secure

Do not confuse a funding mechanism with a funded plan. A funding mechanism provides the financial resources behind a plan, and a plan can be funded or unfunded. The six mechanisms below are listed from the least secure for the executive to the most secure.

Constructive receipt. Whether an executive owes income tax now or later turns on the doctrine of constructive receipt: if the money is available without substantial restriction, limitation, or risk of forfeiture, it is taxed now. For unfunded plans, executives generally pay income tax when payments start. The employer usually deducts its cost when it pays. Section 409A adds its own rules on top.

Part Five: Stock-based pay

Options, restricted stock, appreciation rights, and phantom stock

Company stock represents equity, an ownership interest. A stock option is a right to buy shares at a set price within a set time. A stock grant is an offering of stock. Exercise is the purchase of stock using options; disposition is the sale of stock; and fair market value is the average of the highest and lowest reported sales prices on a given date.

Corrected How options are taxed. The textbook says nonstatutory options are taxed at the time of grant, at ordinary rates, with no tax at exercise. The IRS describes it differently. For a typical employee option, nothing is taxed at grant. When a nonstatutory option is exercised, the spread between the exercise price and the market value is taxed as ordinary income, treated as wages. For an incentive stock option, nothing is taxed at grant or (under the regular tax) at exercise; the alternative minimum tax may apply at exercise; and the gain is taxed as capital gain at sale if the shares are held at least two years from grant and one year from exercise. Otherwise the spread is ordinary income.

Three other stock-based plans

Restricted stock and restricted stock units

The company grants stock (or options) at market or discounted value, and the executive has no control over disposing of it during the vesting period, often many years. Restricted stock units are shares awarded at the end of the restriction. A performance criterion added to the vesting period makes it a performance plan.

Stock appreciation rights

Provide income at the end of a designated period, based on the rise in stock price from the grant date. Executives never have to buy the stock to receive the income, and no one pays tax at grant. The executive pays tax when rights are exercised, and the employer gets a matching deduction.

Phantom stock

A promise by the board to pay a bonus equal to the value, or the increase in value, of company shares. The executive must generally stay employed for several years and then retire. No real shares are ever issued.

Corrected Phantom stock is ordinary income. The textbook says executives pay tax on "capital gains" after converting phantom shares to real ones. Phantom stock payouts are generally taxed as ordinary compensation income when paid, unlike actual stock, whose gains may qualify for capital gains rates. Because phantom stock is deferred compensation, it must also follow Section 409A.

Employee stock purchase plans

A company may offer a formal employee stock purchase plan that lets employees buy stock after a set period. Employees set money aside through payroll deduction during an offering period. A plan can be qualified or nonqualified; to qualify for favorable tax treatment it must meet several criteria, such as allowing all full-time employees with two or more years of service to participate.

Part Six: Leaving the company

Separation agreements: golden parachutes, platinum parachutes, and clawbacks

A separation agreement sets the pay and benefits an executive will receive if the board ends the relationship. It is negotiated during hiring, approved by the board, and put in a written contract.

Golden parachutes

Golden parachutes pay executives after termination caused by a change in ownership or takeover. Boards include them for three reasons: they limit executives' risk in unforeseeable events, they help recruit and retain talent, and they reduce the chance that a CEO will fight a takeover that would benefit the company just to save his or her job. Companies once treated the payments as deductible business expenses, and total values grew far past executives' annual pay, which led to government action.

Updated The tax penalty. Under Sections 280G and 4999, if a change-in-control payment package equals or exceeds three times the executive's base amount, the company loses its deduction for the amount above one times the base amount, and the executive owes a 20% excise tax on the same amount, on top of ordinary income tax. It is a cliff: one dollar over the line puts the whole excess at stake. Some executives negotiate a "gross-up" that has the company pay the excise tax.

Platinum parachutes

CEOs are often terminated before their contracts end after poor performance. Platinum parachutes are lucrative awards, including severance pay, continued benefits, and even stock options, that companies use to avoid long legal battles and negative publicity by paying a CEO to step aside.

Clawbacks

Clawback provisions let a board take back performance-based compensation if the goals were not really achieved. They became common because of public and shareholder scrutiny of CEO pay. The textbook's example is McDonald's effort to recover a $40 million severance payment from CEO Stephen Easterbrook.

Updated How that example ended, and the new rule. In December 2021 Easterbrook settled by returning cash and equity worth more than $105 million, and in 2023 the SEC fined him $400,000 and barred him from serving as an officer or director of an SEC-reporting company. More broadly, the SEC's Rule 10D-1, carried out through NYSE and Nasdaq listing standards effective October 2, 2023, now requires every listed company to adopt a clawback policy. After an accounting restatement, the company must recover incentive pay that current or former executive officers received in the three fiscal years before the restatement, to the extent it exceeds what they would have received on the corrected numbers. Recovery is no-fault: it applies whether or not the executive did anything wrong. It covers pay tied to financial-reporting measures, not pay based only on staying employed.

Part Seven: Sunlight

Reporting and disclosure

Executive pay is unusual because the public can see it. Two laws create the requirements: the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 (Dodd-Frank).

The Securities Exchange Act of 1934

Companies that sell or exchange securities on public exchanges must file information with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), including executive compensation and benefits. For pay, the vehicle is the Definitive Proxy Statement, called a DEF 14A, which reveals compensation for the CEO and the named executive officers (NEOs). The narrative is the Compensation Discussion and Analysis (CD&A). The key table is the Summary Compensation Table, which shows pay over three years: annual compensation (salary, bonus, other), long-term compensation (restricted stock awards, stock appreciation rights, long-term incentive payouts), and an "All Other Compensation" column. Since 2008 and 2009, the SEC has required totals, changes in pension value and nonqualified deferred pay, the present value of accumulated pension benefits, and details of severance and change-in-control agreements.

Dodd-Frank: three provisions

Say-on-pay

Shareholders vote yes or no on executive pay, including golden parachute agreements. The vote is nonbinding but signals concerns to the board.

Independent compensation committees

Independence requirements apply to committee members and their advisors, such as compensation consultants and legal counsel.

Golden parachute disclosure

Companies must disclose the circumstances in which an executive would benefit from a golden parachute, including agreements between an acquirer and the target's executives.

Critical thinking

A new CEO's ask: three promises and a trust

The situation. A NYSE-listed company's compensation committee is hiring an outside CEO. The candidate's term sheet asks for: (1) a SERP paying 60% of her highest three-year average pay after only five years of service; (2) a golden parachute of three times salary and bonus, with a gross-up so she owes no excise tax; (3) the SERP funded through a rabbi trust, "so I can't lose it if we're acquired"; (4) $6 million a year in restricted stock units and options; and (5) no clawback beyond what the law requires.

  1. Which parts of the package shift risk from the executive to the shareholders, and which shift it the other way?
  2. Identify at least two legal or tax problems in the term sheet and say how you would address them.
  3. What information would you gather before the board approves the package, and what result, such as a poor say-on-pay vote, would make you revisit it?

Your response stays in your browser. It is not saved or submitted. Copy it into your course platform when you are done.

There is no single right answer. A strong response takes a position and shows it has weighed these points:

  • The rabbi trust does not do what she thinks. A rabbi trust is an unfunded arrangement: in bankruptcy the company must release its assets to creditors. It provides comfort, not security. Only funded arrangements, such as a secular trust or an executive-owned annuity, are out of the company's creditors' reach, and they can create earlier tax for her.
  • Section 409A. The SERP, the parachute, and any equity award that defers pay must follow strict rules on elections and payment events. A violation puts income tax, a 20% additional tax, and interest on her. A strong answer says counsel must review the documents.
  • Sections 280G and 4999. A parachute of three times pay could cross the three-times-base-amount line. If it does, the company loses its deduction on the excess and she owes a 20% excise tax. A gross-up makes the company pay the tax, compounding the cost and the optics.
  • Clawbacks. Under SEC Rule 10D-1 the company must have a no-fault recovery policy for incentive pay after a restatement. She cannot bargain her way out of it. A stronger answer might add a discretionary clawback for misconduct as well, so a board is not left to sue after the fact, as McDonald's had to.
  • Who bears the risk? The SERP after five years pays a lifetime benefit for short service, which shifts risk to shareholders. RSUs and options tie some of her pay to the share price, which shifts some risk to her. Performance conditions on the equity align it with results.
  • Shareholder reaction. Say-on-pay is nonbinding but public. Executive pay is a topic shareholders react to, and disclosure of the package will be in the proxy. Benchmark against peers and consider what the company's disclosure would say.
  • What would change your mind. Name a result in advance: for example, peer benchmarking showing the package above the 75th percentile without performance conditions, or advice from counsel that a term violates 409A.
