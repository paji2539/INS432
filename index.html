Article I · Plan Overview

Catalog Description & Course Overview

Study of group approach to providing life, health, disability, and retirement plans. New developments in employee benefits, plan design, administration, cost, funding, regulation, and tax considerations. Prerequisites: INS 340.

Why this plan matters

In today's competitive workforce environment, employee benefits serve as a strategic tool, constituting approximately one-third of the total compensation package. This course explores the dynamic landscape of employee benefits from both the employee's and employer's perspectives — health plans, retirement options, paid time off, and the perks that shape financial security and well-being.

On the employer side, attracting and retaining talent depends on the strategic design and management of benefit programs, aligned with legal requirements and diverse employee needs.

Article II · Eligibility & Learning Objectives

What Completing This Plan Gets You

Upon completion of this course, students will acquire an understanding of:

  1. 01Various employee benefits and how they are funded
  2. 02Tax implications of each employee benefit for both employees and employers
  3. 03Legal and regulatory issues regarding employee benefits practices and programs in the U.S.
  4. 04The economic rationales for employers to offer a comprehensive benefits package
  5. 05Pros and cons of different retirement plans for both employees and employers
  6. 06Different types of group health insurance programs and their characteristics
  7. 07How group insurance works in employee benefit programs
  8. 08Ethical and global business issues related to designing, operating, and managing employee benefits for employees traveling to, dispatched to, or hired overseas

Specific Outcomes for 432 Students

By the end of the semester, students should be able to:

  1. Collect information about employee benefit programs and evaluate employee benefit programs offered by organizations;
  2. Perform retirement benefit analysis under various assumptions for employed and self-employed individuals;
  3. Identify the best employee benefit combination to maximize their compensation, considering employee benefits of various types and their family / financial / career status; and
  4. Communicate effectively with benefit specialists, family members, colleagues, friends, and others about various benefit programs and recent changes in regulations and/or benefits.

Specific Outcomes for 532 Students

In addition to the outcomes above for 432 students, by the end of the semester, students should be able to:

  1. Perform SWOT analyses on organizations' employee benefit programs from both employee and employer perspectives; and
  2. Consult with self-employed individuals to strategically design employee benefit programs for both individual self-employed scenarios and those with employees, considering individual needs and organizational goals.

Article V · Coverage Details

Assignments & Exams

What each line item on your Benefits Statement actually covers.

Reaction Papers

120 pts · max 10 pts each

Intended to promote analysis, evaluation, and synthesis of information — and to prompt critical engagement with course material. Read course-related articles or news/media regularly and submit a weekly reaction paper (one per week, first 14 weeks): a concise summary plus your thoughts and critical analysis of a key issue. Sources should be from the past 12 months, align with your interests, and preferably relate to that week's module.

Canvas Discussion

100 pts

Engaging with discussion topics is a highly effective distance-learning method: analyze different perspectives, articulate your viewpoint, and respond to classmates. This sharpens written communication and collaboration while building a sense of community in a virtual environment.

Group Assignment

100 pts

Each group chooses a real organization to thoroughly analyze its employee benefit programs, including a SWOT analysis — strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats present in the benefit offerings. No length requirement, but the report must show comprehensive understanding and thoughtful insight. Each group presents its findings briefly to the class.

Chapter Quizzes & Other Reading Quizzes

220 + 80 pts

Quizzes serve as both learning and assessment tools for chapter content and supplementary materials, mixing true/false, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions. Of the 12 chapter quizzes, the highest 11 count toward your grade.

Final Exam

100 pts

Comprehensive — covering all material from the semester, with a mix of multiple-choice, open-ended, and case analysis questions.

Honesty & Academic Integrity

It is assumed that all students have read and understand ISU's Academic Integrity Policy, Non-Academic Policies and Prohibited Conduct, and Student Rights and Complaint Resolution Procedures, among others, from the Code of Student Conduct. Infractions may result in various actions as outlined in the policy.

It is your responsibility to understand what constitutes plagiarism within an academic setting. Any involvement in plagiarism or other academic dishonesty in written assignments, quizzes, or the final exam may result in an "F" for the course. Any plagiarism in a written assignment results in a zero grade; for a group assignment, plagiarism by one member also zeroes the group assignment for members who did not plagiarize.

According to the Council of Writing Program Administrators (WPA), plagiarism is defined as:

In an instructional setting, plagiarism occurs when a writer deliberately uses someone else's language, ideas, or other original (not common-knowledge) material without acknowledging its source. This definition applies to texts published in print or online, to manuscripts, and to the work of other student writers.

Most current discussions of plagiarism fail to distinguish between (1) submitting someone else's text as one's own, or blurring the line between one's own ideas/words and those borrowed from a source, and (2) carelessly or inadequately citing borrowed ideas and words. Such discussions conflate plagiarism with the misuse of sources.

Ethical writers make every effort to acknowledge sources fully and appropriately. A student who attempts — even clumsily — to identify and credit a source, but misuses a citation format or misuses quotation marks, has not plagiarized; rather, they have failed to cite and document sources appropriately.

Source: Defining and Avoiding Plagiarism — The WPA Statement on Best Practices (wpacouncil.org/node/9)
